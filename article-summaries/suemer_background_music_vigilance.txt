Article Date: 2025, vol 51 (12), pp. 1691-1707

## Summary

Sümer, Serfas, Büttner, and Büttner examine whether listening to music during a long, boring task helps or hurts performance. The task they study is a vigilance task, which requires a person to stay alert for rare targets over an extended period. Performance on these tasks reliably declines over time, a pattern known as the vigilance decrement. Overload theories attribute this decline to depleted cognitive resources, while underload theories attribute it to boredom and disengagement. The authors argue that these two views have often confused arousal with boredom, and they use background music to separate the two.

The article reports two preregistered experiments conducted at a German university between 2022 and 2024. In Study 1, 120 undergraduates completed a 12-minute computerized task in which they pressed a key when a target letter appeared and withheld a response for similar-looking nontargets. Half listened to four popular songs from the 2021 German charts through headphones, selected in a pilot study for being both familiar and liked, and half worked in silence. Arousal was measured before and after the task, and participants also completed measures of extraversion and boredom proneness. The authors predicted that music would improve performance, that arousal would explain the effect, and that extraverts and people prone to boredom would benefit most. None of these predictions held up. The two groups did not differ in correct detections or false alarms, and a Bayesian analysis indicated the data supported the null hypothesis. The music group did report higher arousal and much lower boredom, but this did not translate into better performance. Performance declined over the 12 minutes in both groups, confirming that the vigilance decrement occurred. Introverts outperformed extraverts, but neither extraversion nor boredom proneness changed the effect of music.

Study 2 shifted the focus to music preference. A new sample of 117 participants was divided into three groups: liked music (the same songs as Study 1), disliked music (German Schlager songs that pilot participants rated strongly negatively), and silence. The authors also measured task load, task engagement, fatigue, and how boring and difficult participants found the task. Once again, the groups did not differ in correct answers or false alarms, and the Bayesian results favored the null. Task load ratings did not differ either, which argues against the idea that music was consuming cognitive resources. What differed was the subjective experience. Both music groups reported higher engagement than the control group, and the liked music group found the task less boring and reported lower boredom afterward. Across all participants, higher boredom was associated with more missed targets in the second half of the task.

The authors conclude that neither theory fully explains their results. Music increased arousal and engagement and reduced boredom, which underload theory says should have helped, yet performance was unchanged. Music also did not increase task load or impair performance, which overload theory would have predicted. They turn instead to the meaning and attentional components model of boredom, which holds that boredom comes from a mismatch in both stimulation and meaning. In their view, music fixed the stimulation problem but not the meaning problem, so people felt better about the task without doing it any better. They acknowledge that a 12-minute task may have been too short and too easy to reveal differences, that the music genres were narrow, and that self-selected music might work differently than music chosen by the experimenters.

## Opinion

Personally, I appreciated that this article reported two null results honestly instead of searching for a positive finding to emphasize. The authors preregistered their hypotheses, every one of them was rejected, and they say so directly. The use of Bayesian analyses to show that the data support the absence of an effect, rather than simply failing to find one, makes the null result more convincing than it otherwise would be.

The most interesting finding to me is the gap between how participants felt and how they performed. Music made the task feel more engaging and less boring, and it raised arousal, but it did nothing for accuracy. This matches my own experience of studying with music, where the time passes more easily without the work obviously getting better. The authors' explanation using the meaning and attentional components model is reasonable, but it was introduced after the fact, and I think they should have been more cautious about presenting a post hoc framework as a theoretical contribution.

The biggest limitation is the task itself. A 12-minute letter detection task is a long way from an hour-long study session or the air traffic control and surveillance jobs the authors mention as applications. The authors admit that longer tasks produce larger vigilance decrements and that their task may have been too easy to show an effect. Given that, the safest conclusion is not that music does not matter, but that it does not matter for a short and simple task. Whether it helps or hurts over a longer and harder one remains open, and the authors' recommendation to vary task difficulty and meaning in future studies is the right next step.
