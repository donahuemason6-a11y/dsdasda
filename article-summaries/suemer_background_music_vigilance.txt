Journal Name: Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance (American Psychological Association)

Article Date: 2025, vol 51 (12), pp. 1691-1707

## Summary

Sümer, Serfas, Büttner, and Büttner examine a question that many students will recognize from their own study habits: does listening to music while doing a long, boring task help or hurt performance? The specific task they are interested in is a vigilance task, which requires a person to stay alert for rare targets over an extended period of time. Performance on these tasks reliably declines over time, which is known as the vigilance decrement. Two competing theories explain this decline. Overload theories argue that the task drains limited cognitive resources. Underload theories argue that the task is so monotonous and under-stimulating that people disengage from it. The authors point out that these two views have often confused arousal with boredom, and they use background music as a way to separate the two.

The article reports two preregistered experiments conducted at a German university between 2022 and 2024. In Study 1, 120 undergraduates completed a 12-minute computerized vigilance task in which they had to press a key when a target letter appeared and withhold a response when similar-looking nontargets appeared. Half of the participants listened to four popular, well-liked songs from the 2021 German charts through headphones, and half completed the task in silence. The songs had been selected in a pilot study for being both familiar and liked. Arousal was measured before and after the task, state boredom was measured after the task, and participants also completed measures of extraversion and boredom proneness. The authors predicted that music would improve performance, that arousal would explain this effect, and that extraverts and people prone to boredom would benefit the most.

None of these predictions held up. The music group and the silent group did not differ in correct detections or in false alarms, and a Bayesian analysis indicated that the data actually supported the null hypothesis. Music did do what it was supposed to do in one respect, since the music group reported higher arousal both before and after the task and much lower boredom afterward. However, this did not translate into better performance. Performance declined across the three 4-minute segments of the task in both groups, confirming that the vigilance decrement occurred. Introverts outperformed extraverts, but neither extraversion nor boredom proneness changed the effect of music.

Study 2 shifted the focus from personality to music preference. A new sample of 117 participants was divided into three groups: liked music (the same songs as in Study 1), disliked music (a playlist of German Schlager songs that pilot participants rated as strongly disliked), and silence. The authors also measured task load, task engagement, fatigue, and how boring and difficult participants found the task. Once again, the three groups did not differ in correct answers or false alarms, and the Bayesian results favored the null. Task load ratings did not differ either, which argues against the idea that music was consuming cognitive resources. What did differ was the subjective experience. Both music groups reported higher task engagement than the control group, and the liked music group found the task less boring and reported lower boredom afterward. Across all participants, higher boredom was associated with more missed targets in the second half of the task.

In the discussion, the authors conclude that neither the overload nor the underload theory fully explains their results. Music increased arousal and engagement and reduced boredom, which underload theory says should have helped, yet performance was unchanged. At the same time, music did not increase task load or impair performance, which overload theory would have predicted. They turn instead to a boredom-based framework called the meaning and attentional components model, which holds that boredom comes from a mismatch in both stimulation and meaning. In their view, music fixed the stimulation problem but not the meaning problem, so people felt better about the task without doing it any better. The authors acknowledge that a 12-minute task may have been too short and too easy to reveal differences, that the music genres were narrow, and that self-selected music might work differently than music chosen by the experimenters.

## Opinion

Personally, I appreciated that this article reported two null results honestly instead of searching for a positive finding to emphasize. The authors preregistered their hypotheses, every one of their main hypotheses was rejected, and they say so directly. The use of Bayesian analyses to show that the data support the absence of an effect, rather than simply failing to find one, makes the null result more convincing than it otherwise would be.

The most interesting finding to me is the gap between how participants felt and how they performed. Music made the task feel more engaging and less boring, and it raised arousal, but it did nothing for accuracy. This matches my own experience of studying with music, where the music makes the time pass more easily without obviously making the work better. The authors' explanation using the meaning and attentional components model is reasonable, but it was introduced after the fact, and I think they should have been more cautious about presenting a post hoc framework as a theoretical contribution.

I also think the biggest limitation is the task itself. A 12-minute letter detection task is a long way from the hour-long study sessions or the air traffic control and surveillance jobs that the authors mention as real-world applications. The authors admit that longer tasks produce larger vigilance decrements and that their task may have been too easy to show an effect. Given that, the safest conclusion from this article is not that music does not matter, but that music does not matter for a short and simple task. Whether it helps or hurts over a longer and harder one remains an open question, and the authors' recommendation to manipulate task difficulty and meaning in future studies is the right next step.
