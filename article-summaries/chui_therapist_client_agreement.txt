Article Authors: Harold Chui, Beatriz Palma, John L. Jackson, and Clara E. Hill
Article Date: 2020, Volume 67, Issue 3, pages 349-360

## Summary

Chui, Palma, Jackson, and Hill start from an old observation in psychotherapy research: therapists and clients often disagree about what made therapy work. Therapists tend to credit their techniques, while clients remember feeling understood. Most prior studies of therapist-client agreement focused on the working alliance and used standardized rating scales, which cannot capture what each person actually found helpful or what they wished had gone differently. This study asked those questions directly and then tested whether agreement predicted outcome.

The data came from a low-fee community clinic run by a psychology department in the Mid-Atlantic United States, where doctoral students provided open-ended psychodynamic and interpersonal therapy. From roughly 200 clients seen over eight years, the authors narrowed the sample to 18 therapist-client pairs in which both people had completed separate post-therapy interviews, the client had at least one outcome assessment after intake, and each therapist contributed only one case. Clients averaged about 29 years old and 49 sessions, with a range from 17 to 181. Therapists were counseling psychology doctoral students with psychodynamic training. Interviews were conducted by a different clinician than the one who had treated the client, usually within two weeks of the final session.

Three judges analyzed the interview transcripts using consensual qualitative research, sorting responses into four domains: what therapists found helpful, what clients found helpful, what therapists wished had been different, and what clients wished had been different. The judges then rated each pair's level of agreement on a five-point scale. Outcome was measured with the Outcome Questionnaire for psychological symptoms and the Inventory of Interpersonal Problems, both completed every eighth session, and analyzed with multilevel modeling to see how quickly scores changed over the course of therapy.

Therapists most often said that exploring the therapeutic relationship itself was helpful. Clients most often said that being supported, validated, and affirmed was helpful, followed closely by the therapist offering new perspectives on their problems. Both groups mentioned the therapist challenging the client and exploring the relationship, though clients named support far more often than therapists did. Agreement on helpful experiences was moderate.

Wishes were a different story. Therapists wished they had had more time, had been more present or confident, had explored other topics such as substance use or body image, or had intervened earlier. Clients overwhelmingly wished for more structure and direction, including homework, role plays, breathing exercises, and clearer explanations of how the therapy worked. Not a single category of wishes overlapped between the two groups, and agreement on wishes was low.

The outcome analysis showed that agreement on what was helpful predicted faster improvement on both the symptom and interpersonal measures across the course of therapy. Agreement on wishes predicted nothing. The authors interpret this to mean that a shared understanding of the tasks of therapy matters more than shared views about what was missing, which fits the broader literature linking collaboration on goals and tasks to good outcomes. They also suggest that clients in open-ended psychodynamic therapy may have expected the kind of structure found in cognitive-behavioral treatment, which argues for therapists checking in periodically about whether the approach matches what the client wants.

## Opinion

The finding I keep returning to is the one about wishes. Fourteen of eighteen clients wanted more structure, and none of their therapists mentioned it. That is not a small mismatch. It suggests these clients sat through dozens of sessions wanting homework and clear explanations while their therapists were focused on being more present. The authors handle this gently, framing it as a reason to check in, but I read it as a stronger indictment of open-ended therapy delivered without a clear rationale.

I also appreciated that the authors did not inflate a modest study. Eighteen pairs from one psychodynamic training clinic is a small sample, and the clients who returned for interviews are probably not representative of those who dropped out. The authors say all of this. The multilevel approach, which used every available outcome score rather than a simple before-and-after difference, is a sensible way to get as much as possible out of a small dataset, and the effect on symptoms held up with baseline severity controlled.

Where I part with the article is its explanation for why clients valued support more than therapists did. The authors suggest support comes so naturally to therapists that they do not notice it. That may be true, but a simpler reading is that trainees were taught to value interpretation and exploration, so that is what they reported. Either way, the practical lesson is the same, and it is a useful one for anyone planning to work in a clinical setting: ask clients what they find helpful, because their answer may not be what you think you are doing well.
