## Summary

Boswell and twelve coauthors, writing in connection with the American Psychological Association's Advisory Committee for Measurement-Based Care, make a policy argument rather than reporting a new experiment. Their claim is that the APA should develop a professional practice guideline for measurement-based care, and the article lays out why such a guideline is needed and what it might say.

Measurement-based care is the routine collection of patient-generated data, usually brief symptom questionnaires, throughout treatment, combined with two further steps: sharing that information with the patient in a timely way, and using it alongside clinical judgment to adjust the treatment plan. The authors separate it from related terms such as routine outcome monitoring and feedback-informed treatment, partly because the lack of shared language has slowed adoption. Despite a large research base, fewer than 20 percent of mental and behavioral health providers report using the practice.

The article also explains what kind of document is being proposed. Clinical practice guidelines recommend treatments for particular disorders. Professional practice guidelines are aspirational statements about how psychologists should conduct themselves in particular roles or settings, and APA policy allows a new one only when there is demonstrated need in three areas: public benefit, professional guidance, and legal or regulatory issues. The bulk of the article walks through each.

On the evidence, the authors summarize ten meta-analyses. Effects of feedback on symptom outcomes range from none to medium, with the largest and most consistent benefits for patients who are not on track to improve. The most recent and largest analysis, covering roughly 22,000 patients, found a small but reliable effect on symptom reduction and a modest reduction in dropout. Studies that paired feedback with clinical support tools, which guide the clinician on what to do when a patient is off track, showed larger effects than feedback alone. Beyond symptoms, the practice is associated with higher patient satisfaction, stronger working alliance, and lower rates of nonresponse and premature termination. The authors also note that clinicians without objective data tend to overestimate their own effectiveness and miss deterioration.

For public benefit, they argue the practice is consistent with patients' rights to participate in evaluating their own care. They also address equity. Two studies found that Latinx or Hispanic providers held less positive attitudes toward outcome measures, often because of language and literacy barriers, and a pediatric psychiatry study found that Medicaid patients were about half as likely to be given access to the measurement system until enrollment was automated. For professional guidance, they point to feedback theory and to a finding that providers assume patients dislike being measured when patient interviews show nearly universal positive attitudes. For legal and regulatory need, they cite the shift toward value-based payment under the 2015 Medicare reauthorization law and the Joint Commission's 2018 standard requiring behavioral health organizations to use measurement-based care. Their concern is that if payers choose the measures without clinician input, reimbursement will reward cost containment rather than quality.

The proposed guideline is a single sentence: psychologists should aim to assess treatment process and outcomes routinely and fold that information into ongoing collaboration with their patients. The authors stress that the practice is a tool to inform judgment, not an algorithm to replace it, and they close with cautions about overconfidence in the data, the validation gaps in commercial digital tools, and the weak evidence that pay-for-performance schemes actually improve outcomes.

## Opinion

I came to this article expecting an advertisement for outcome questionnaires and found something more careful. The authors are honest that the average effect of feedback is small, and they do not oversell it. Their stronger argument is the one about patients who are not on track, paired with the finding that clinicians rate their own effectiveness far above average. If therapists cannot reliably tell which clients are getting worse, then a brief questionnaire every session is a cheap safeguard, and the case for it does not depend on large average effects.

What I found most useful was the section on barriers, because it explains the 20 percent uptake figure without blaming clinicians. Many settings have no electronic health record that scores and graphs the results, no one trained to interpret them, and no payer willing to pay for the time. A guideline cannot fix those problems, and the authors admit that. I think they are right that it would still shift the default, in the same way the Joint Commission standard already has for accredited organizations.

The part that gave me pause is the legal and regulatory section. The authors want psychologists at the table when payers decide what gets measured, which is reasonable, but it also means the profession is partly asking for a guideline so that it can shape the metrics it will be judged by. That is not a bad motive, but it is a different motive from patient benefit, and the article would be stronger if it said so directly. Even with that reservation, the equity findings about Medicaid patients and language barriers convinced me that measurement done well serves the patients who are usually served worst.
