Article Name: With a Little Help From My Playlists: The Impact of Background Music on Sustained Attention Performance
Journal Name: Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance (American Psychological Association)
Article Authors: Cansu Sümer, Benjamin G. Serfas, Kim Büttner, and Oliver B. Büttner
Article Date: 2025, vol 51 (12), pp. 1691-1707

## Summary

Sümer et al. explore whether listening to music while performing a long, boring task impacts task performance. The article starts by describing a vigilance task, which requires participants to remain vigilant for rare targets for an extended period. People’s performance on such tasks tends to decline over time, referred to as the vigilance decrement. Overload theories posit that this is because participants run out of cognitive resources, while underload theories suggest that this decline is due to boredom and disengagement. The authors argue that existing theories have conflated arousal with boredom, and they investigate how listening to background music affects these factors.

The article reports two preregistered experiments carried out in a German university between 2022 and 2024. In study 1, 120 undergraduates completed a 12-minute, computerised task, in which they pressed a key when they saw a target letter and withheld their response for similar-looking nontargets. Half of the participants listened to four popular songs, taken from the top German charts in 2021, selected in a pilot study as familiar and liked. The other half of participants performed the task in silence. Participants’ arousal levels were measured before and after the task, and they also completed a measure of extraversion and boredom proneness. The authors hypothesised that listening to music would improve performance, that this effect would be mediated by arousal, and that the effect would be strongest for extraverted people and for those who are particularly prone to boredom. However, these hypotheses were not supported. There were no differences between the two groups in the number of correctly detected targets or false alarms. A Bayesian analysis suggested that the data supported the null hypothesis. Participants in the music condition reported higher arousal and much lower boredom than the other group, but this did not lead to improved performance. Overall, both groups’ performance declined over the 12 minutes, showing that the vigilance decrement occurred. Introverts performed better than extraverts, but extraversion and boredom proneness did not moderate the effects of music.

In study 2, the authors focused on the role of participants’ music preference. They recruited a new sample of 117 participants and divided them into three groups: the same songs as in study 1 (liked music), songs that participants disliked, and a control group (silence). The songs that participants disliked were German Schlager songs, which participants rated highly negatively in a pilot study. The authors also assessed participants’ task load, task engagement, fatigue, and how boring and difficult they found the task. Again, they found no differences between the groups in the number of correct answers or false alarms, and Bayesian analyses indicated that these data supported the null hypothesis. There were no differences between the groups in their task load ratings, suggesting that listening to music did not consume cognitive resources. However, participants in both music groups did report higher task engagement than the control group. They also found the task less boring and reported lower boredom after the task if they listened to liked music. Participants who reported higher boredom also missed more targets in the second half of the task.

The authors argue that neither underload nor overload theory can fully explain the results. On the one hand, listening to music increased arousal and engagement, and reduced boredom, which should have improved performance according to underload theory, but performance was not affected. On the other hand, listening to music did not increase task load, and did not negatively affect task performance as might be expected if it was consuming cognitive resources. The authors instead propose an explanation based on the meaning and attentional components model of boredom, which suggests that boredom results from a mismatch in stimulation and meaning. They suggest that listening to music alleviated the problem of stimulation, but not the problem of meaning, and this is why participants enjoyed the task more, but did not perform better. They note that 12 minutes may not have been long enough, and that the task might have been too easy for differences to appear, as well as the fact that only a narrow range of music genres was investigated. They also note that self-selected music might have different effects than the experimenter-chosen music they used.

## Opinion

I enjoyed that this article reported two null results honestly, rather than finding a way to focus on a positive result. The authors preregistered their hypotheses and rejected all of them. They also used Bayesian analyses to show that the data supported the null hypothesis, rather than simply failing to support their hypotheses, making the null result more convincing.

Personally, I found it most interesting that listening to music made people feel more engaged and less bored, and increased their arousal, but did not have any effect on task performance. This matches my own experiences, where listening to music makes the time go faster when I am studying but I do not notice any difference in performance. Their explanation based on the meaning and attentional components model of boredom is an interesting proposal, but the authors only introduce this theory post hoc, so they should be more careful in how they present it.

The main limitation of this study, in my view, is the task itself. A 12-minute letter detection task is quite removed from the sort of tasks that the authors discuss as real-world applications (e.g. an hour-long study session, or jobs like air traffic controller or surveillance operator). The authors themselves note that the vigilance decrement is larger when tasks are longer, and their task may have been too easy, or too short, to demonstrate any effect. It would therefore be safer to conclude that listening to music does not have any effect on task performance, as long as the task is short and easy. However, the question of whether it has any effect on longer, more difficult tasks remains open, and I think the authors’ suggestion that future studies should vary task difficulty and meaning is a good one.
