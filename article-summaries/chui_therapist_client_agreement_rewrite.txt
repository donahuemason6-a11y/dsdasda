Article Name: Therapist–Client Agreement on Helpful and Wished-For Experiences in Psychotherapy: Associations With Outcome
Journal Name: Journal of Counseling Psychology (American Psychological Association)
Article Authors: Harold Chui, Beatriz Palma, John L. Jackson, and Clara E. Hill
Article Date: 2020, vol 67 (3), pp. 349-360

## Summary

The notion that therapists and clients disagree on what makes psychotherapy helpful is not a new one. Therapists tend to think the helpful elements are their techniques while clients see being understood as helpful. Most previous research on therapist-client agreement has been on the working alliance, with data from standardized rating scales that do not get at what clients and therapists found helpful or would have wished to be different. Chui, Palma, Jackson, and Hill address these two issues and then test if the amount of therapist-client agreement is related to outcome.

The data for this study were from a low-fee community clinic affiliated with a psychology department in the Mid-Atlantic United States. The clinic provides open-ended psychodynamic and interpersonal therapy by doctoral students. Out of the approximately 200 clients seen at the clinic, 75 therapist-client pairs had post-therapy interviews separately conducted, and 18 pairs were selected that also had at least one outcome assessment other than the initial intake assessment, with each therapist only having one client in the study. The average client was about 29 years old and had 49 therapy sessions (range 17-181). The therapists were all doctoral students in counseling psychology and were trained in psychodynamic psychotherapy. The interviewers were different clinicians than the therapists and conducted the interviews within 2 weeks after the client completed therapy.

The interview data were analyzed by three judges who used a consensual qualitative research approach. The interviews were coded into 4 domains: therapist helpfulness, client helpfulness, therapist wishes, and client wishes. These judges also rated the level of agreement in each domain for each therapist-client pair on a scale from 1 to 5. Outcome was assessed using the Outcome Questionnaire (for psychological symptoms) and the Inventory of Interpersonal Problems. These were administered every 8th session. Outcome data were analyzed using multilevel modeling and focused on how quickly the measures changed over the course of therapy.

Therapists most frequently said that exploring the therapeutic relationship itself was helpful. Clients most frequently said that being supported, validated, and affirmed was helpful, followed closely by when therapists gave them new perspectives on their problems. Both therapists and clients frequently said that therapist challenge and exploring the therapeutic relationship were helpful. However, clients much more frequently than therapists found support to be helpful. The level of therapist-client agreement on helpful experiences was moderate. Therapists most frequently wished that they had more time, been more present/confident, discussed other topics (e.g., substance use, body image), or intervened sooner. Clients overwhelmingly wished that their therapy had been more structured and directive (e.g., more homework, role plays, breathing exercises, and clearer explanations for how therapy works). No categories of wishes overlapped between therapists and clients, and the level of agreement on wishes was low.

The outcome analyses showed that agreement on helpful experiences predicted faster improvement on both the symptoms and interpersonal measures during the course of therapy. Agreement on wishes predicted nothing. The authors interpret this as suggesting that a shared understanding of the task of therapy is more important than a shared understanding of what was missing, which is consistent with other studies that have shown that a collaborative relationship around goals and tasks is associated with good outcome. They also suggest that clients in open-ended psychodynamic therapy may have had expectations that were similar to a cognitive-behavioral treatment, since these clients reported wanting a more structured approach. They recommend that therapists regularly check in with their clients about whether their approach is matching the client's desire.

## Opinion

My favorite finding in this study was that of the 18 clients, 14 of them wished for more structure in their therapy, whereas none of their therapists mentioned structure. That means that these clients were going to therapy, probably thinking they would get homework and explanations for how therapy works, but their therapists were mostly concerned with being more present. The authors are gentle with this finding, saying it may be a reason for therapists to check in with their clients. I think the finding is a bit more of an indictment of open-ended therapy in the absence of a clear rationale.

I am glad to see that the authors did not inflate their modest study. The sample is small (18 therapist-client pairs), they were all from one psychodynamic training clinic, and the clients who completed the interview are probably not representative of all clients (since many clients dropped out). The authors address these limitations. The way they analyzed their data using multilevel modeling is sensible, since they could make the most of a small data set by using every outcome measure, rather than simply subtracting the posttest from the pretest. They also showed that the effect on symptoms remained significant even after controlling for baseline symptom severity.

I do not agree with the authors' explanation of why clients found support more helpful than therapists did. The authors suggest it is because giving support comes so naturally to therapists that they do not notice it. I think a simpler interpretation is that these trainees were trained to value interpretation and exploration in therapy, which is why they reported that these were helpful. Either way, I take the practical take-home from this article to be that therapists should ask their clients what is helpful, since the client's answer may not be what they think is helpful, which is a useful lesson for someone who plans to work in a clinical setting.
