## Summary

The article by Boswell et al. is not a research study in the traditional sense. Instead, it is a policy argument written in connection with the American Psychological Association's Advisory Committee for Measurement-Based Care. The authors argue that the APA should develop a professional practice guideline for measurement-based care, and the article explains why such a guideline is needed and what it could say.

Measurement-based care refers to the routine collection of patient-generated data, usually brief symptom questionnaires, over the course of treatment. According to Boswell et al., it consists of three elements: collecting the data on a regular basis, sharing the results with the patient in a timely manner, and using the results along with clinical judgment to adjust the treatment plan. The authors distinguish measurement-based care from similar terms such as routine outcome monitoring and feedback-informed treatment. They note that the lack of a shared vocabulary has been one of the barriers to adoption. Despite a large body of supporting research, less than 20% of mental and behavioral health providers report using measurement-based care.

Boswell et al. also explain the difference between clinical practice guidelines and professional practice guidelines. Clinical practice guidelines recommend treatments for specific disorders. Professional practice guidelines are aspirational statements regarding how psychologists should practice in particular roles, settings, or populations. According to APA policy, a new professional practice guideline can only be developed when there is a demonstrated need in three domains: public benefit, professional guidance, and legal and regulatory issues. The majority of the article addresses each of these domains in turn.

In terms of the evidence, the authors summarize ten meta-analyses. The effects of feedback on symptom outcomes ranged from no effect to medium effects, with the largest effects found for patients who were not on track to improve. The most recent and largest meta-analysis, which included approximately 22,000 patients, found a small but statistically significant effect on symptom reduction and a small reduction in dropout rates. Studies that combined feedback with clinical support tools, which provide the clinician with guidance on what to do when a patient is not progressing, produced larger effects than feedback alone. In addition to symptom reduction, measurement-based care is associated with increased patient satisfaction, a stronger working alliance, and reduced rates of nonresponse and premature termination. The authors also note that without objective data, clinicians tend to overestimate their own effectiveness and fail to recognize when patients are deteriorating.

Regarding public benefit, the authors argue that measurement-based care is consistent with patients' rights to participate in evaluating their own care. They also address equity. Two studies found that Latinx or Hispanic providers held less positive attitudes toward outcome measures, largely due to concerns regarding language and literacy barriers. Additionally, a study in a pediatric psychiatry department found that Medicaid patients were approximately half as likely to be given access to the measurement system until the enrollment process was automated. Regarding professional guidance, the authors point to feedback theory as well as a study which found that providers assumed patients viewed measurement negatively, whereas interviews with patients revealed almost universally positive attitudes. Regarding legal and regulatory issues, the authors cite the shift toward value-based payment under the 2015 Medicare reauthorization legislation and the Joint Commission's 2018 standard requiring behavioral health organizations to implement measurement-based care. The authors express concern that if payers select the measures without input from clinicians, reimbursement models will reward cost containment rather than quality of care.

The proposed guideline statement itself is a single sentence stating that psychologists should aim to routinely assess the process and outcomes of treatment and integrate that information into ongoing collaboration with their patients. The authors emphasize that measurement-based care is a tool meant to inform clinical judgment rather than an algorithm meant to replace it. They conclude with several cautions, including the risk of overconfidence in the data, the lack of validation for many commercial digital tools, and the weak evidence that pay-for-performance programs actually improve patient outcomes.

## Opinion

Personally, I expected this article to be a one-sided promotion of outcome questionnaires, but it turned out to be more balanced than that. The authors openly acknowledge that the average effect of feedback is small, and they do not overstate the findings. In my view, their strongest argument concerns patients who are not on track, especially when combined with the finding that clinicians rate their own effectiveness far above average. If therapists cannot reliably identify which clients are getting worse, then a brief questionnaire at each session is a low-cost safeguard, and the justification for it does not depend on large average effects.

I found the section on barriers to be the most useful part of the article because it explains the low adoption rate without blaming clinicians. Many settings do not have an electronic health record that scores and graphs the results, do not have staff trained to interpret them, and do not have payers willing to reimburse for the time involved. A guideline cannot solve these problems, and the authors admit as much. However, I agree with them that a guideline would still change the default expectation, similar to what the Joint Commission standard has already done for accredited organizations.

The legal and regulatory section is the one I have the most reservations about. The authors want psychologists to be involved when payers decide what will be measured, which is reasonable. At the same time, this means the profession is partly requesting a guideline in order to shape the metrics it will be evaluated on. That is not an unreasonable motive, but it is a different motive from patient benefit, and I believe the article would be stronger if it acknowledged this more directly. Despite this reservation, the findings regarding Medicaid patients and language barriers convinced me that measurement-based care, when implemented properly, benefits the patients who typically receive the lowest quality of care.
