## Summary

In an article in an American Psychological Association journal, Boswell and 12 co-authors make a policy argument in connection with the Association's Advisory Committee for Measurement-Based Care. Instead of reporting a new experiment, they argue a position. Their argument is that the APA needs to create a professional practice guideline for measurement-based care, and the article sets out the rationale for such a guideline, why it is required, and the statement it could provide.

Measurement-based care is the regular gathering of patient-provided data, typically short symptom questionnaires, during treatment, plus two additional steps: delivery of that information to the patient in a timely fashion, and applying it in conjunction with clinical judgment to make adjustments to the treatment plan. The authors distinguish it from similar terms like routine outcome monitoring and feedback-informed treatment, notably because the absence of common language has slowed adoption. Although there is a large research base, less than 20 per cent of mental and behavioral health providers report using the practice.

A description of the proposed document type is also included in the article. Clinical practice guidelines are recommendations for treatments of specific disorders. Professional practice guidelines are aspirational statements about how psychologists should conduct themselves in particular roles or settings, and APA policy says that a new one can be added only if there is a clear need in three areas. These areas include public benefit, professional guidance and legal/regulatory matters. The bulk of the article goes through them one by one.

On the evidence, the authors' summary covers ten meta-analyses. The influence of feedback on symptom outcomes varies from none to medium, with the greatest and most consistent benefits for patients who don't show signs of improvement. In the most recent and largest analysis, covering about 22,000 patients, they identified a small but dependable effect in reducing symptoms and a moderate reduction in dropout. Studies that coupled feedback with clinical support tools, which guide the clinician on what to do when a patient is off track, obtained larger effects than feedback alone. In addition to symptoms, the practice is also linked to greater satisfaction with the care received, better alliance, and decreased nonresponse and premature termination rates. The authors also mention that the absence of objective data leads clinicians to overestimate their efficacy and overlook deterioration.

According to them, the practice is in the public interest and in line with patients' rights to participate in assessing their own care. They also tackle the issues of equity. Two studies indicated that Latinx or Hispanic providers had less positive attitudes toward outcome measures, in part because of language and literacy barriers, and a study of pediatric psychiatric patients revealed that Medicaid patients were about half as likely to be given access to the measurement system until enrollment was automated. For professional guidance, they refer to feedback theory and to a result that providers assume patients do not like being measured, when almost all patient interviews reveal universal positive attitudes. For legal and regulatory need, they refer to the transition towards value-based payment under the 2015 reauthorization of the Medicare Act and the Joint Commission's 2018 standard for behavioral health organizations to implement measurement-based care. Their concern is that if the specific measurements are chosen by the payers, they may not be the most relevant to the clinical care of the patient, and reimbursement will focus on saving money, not quality.

The proposed guideline is one sentence: psychologists aim to track the process and outcomes of treatment regularly and incorporate that information into an ongoing collaborative process with their patients. The authors emphasize that the practice should be used as a means of informing judgement, not an algorithm to replace it, and they close with caution about over-relying on the data, the lack of validation in commercial digital tools, and the limited evidence that pay-for-performance schemes are having a positive impact.

## Opinion

I found myself reading this article with the expectation that it would be an ad for outcome questionnaires, but it was something more careful. The authors are straight-forwardly admitting that the impact of feedback is not huge, and they don't over sell it. Their better argument is for patients who aren't on track, in combination with the fact that clinicians self-rate their effectiveness much higher than the average. If therapists are not able to accurately determine which clients are deteriorating, then a short questionnaire every session is a relatively low cost protection, and the case for it does not rely on large average effects.

What I found most helpful was the section on barriers, which explains the 20 percent uptake figure without faulting clinicians. There are many environments where there is no electronic health record that scores and graphs the results, no one trained to interpret them, and no payer willing to pay for the time. Those problems a guideline can't resolve, as the authors say. I think they are right that it would still change the default, as the Joint Commission standard already has for accredited organizations.

The section that struck me is the legal/regulatory one. The authors want psychologists at the table when the payers determine what gets measured, which is reasonable, but it also implies that the profession is requesting a guideline as a way to define the dimensions it will be judged by. That is not a bad reason, but it is a different reason from what is best for the patient, and the article would be better if it expressed that directly. Despite this limitation, the equity results about Medicaid patients and language barriers convinced me that measurement done well serves the patients that are typically served worst.
