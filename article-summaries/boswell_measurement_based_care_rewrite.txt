## Summary

Boswell and 12 other authors published an article where they make a policy argument for developing a professional practice guideline for measurement-based care by the American Psychological Association. The article makes a case for the need and content of a professional practice guideline for measurement-based care. It is a work prepared in connection with the Advisory Committee for Measurement-Based Care of the American Psychological Association.

Measurement-based care (MBC) involves the routine collection of patient-generated data, typically brief symptom questionnaires, during the course of treatment. It also involves the timely sharing of this information with the patient and the use of this information to inform treatment plan decisions, in addition to the clinician's judgment. The authors distinguish MBC from similar-sounding terms like routine outcome monitoring and feedback-informed treatment. Part of the reason why MBC has not been widely adopted is the lack of a shared understanding. Despite the large research base on MBC, less than 20% of providers in mental and behavioral health reported using it.

The article also goes on to explain what a professional practice guideline entails. A clinical practice guideline, on the other hand, is a guideline that recommends the treatment of particular disorders. A professional practice guideline, by contrast, is a statement of best practices or aspirational standards for how a psychologist should behave in a particular role or setting. The APA allows a new professional practice guideline to be developed only when there is a demonstrated need in three areas: public benefit, guidance to the profession, and legal or regulatory issues. The rest of the article goes on to make a case for the need to develop a professional practice guideline for MBC in these three areas.

The authors summarize findings from 10 meta-analyses. The effects of MBC, or feedback, on symptom outcomes range from no effect to a medium effect. The benefits of feedback are larger and more consistent for patients who are not on track to improve. The most recent and largest meta-analysis, which included nearly 22,000 patients, found a small but reliable effect of feedback on reducing symptoms and a modest reduction in dropout rates. This effect was larger when feedback was paired with clinical support tools, which advise the clinician on how to change the treatment plan when a patient is not on track to improve. In addition to improving symptom outcomes, MBC is associated with greater patient satisfaction, greater working alliance, and reduced rates of nonresponse and premature treatment termination. The authors also note that clinicians tend to overestimate their effectiveness when they do not have access to objective data, and that clinicians are not adept at identifying worsening patients.

With regard to public benefit, the authors note that MBC is consistent with patients' right to be part of evaluating treatment outcomes. In terms of equity, two studies found that Latinx or Hispanic providers had less positive attitudes toward the use of outcome measures, possibly due to language and literacy barriers. Similarly, a study in pediatric psychiatry found that patients on Medicaid were about half as likely to have access to the MBC system as their counterparts until enrollment in the system was automated. The authors also make the case for a MBC professional practice guideline from the perspective of professional guidance. They point to the theory of feedback and the finding that providers believe patients do not like to be measured, despite interviews with patients showing near-universal positive attitudes toward MBC. Finally, in the legal or regulatory area, the authors cite the shift toward value-based payment in the healthcare industry following the reauthorization of Medicare in 2015. Additionally, the Joint Commission issued a standard in 2018 requiring behavioral health organizations to adopt MBC. The authors are concerned that if payers are allowed to choose the outcome measures without input from clinicians, then reimbursement schemes will be oriented toward cost containment rather than quality.

The proposed guideline is short: psychologists should seek to assess treatment process and outcome information routinely and use that information to inform their ongoing collaborative work with patients. The authors make it clear that MBC is a tool to assist the clinician in making judgments, rather than an algorithmic replacement for the clinician's judgment. The article also includes cautions about potential overconfidence in MBC data and about the lack of validation in some commercial digital measurement tools. Finally, the authors note the lack of evidence that pay-for-performance schemes can actually improve outcomes.

## Opinion

I appreciated that this article is not simply an advertisement for the use of outcome measures. In fact, the authors are honest in stating that the average effect of feedback is relatively small. However, the authors are able to make a case for MBC based on the evidence that the benefits of feedback are larger for patients who are not on track to improve, as well as the evidence that clinicians rate their own effectiveness as much higher than average. Thus, the case for MBC does not depend on showing large average effects. Instead, the authors show that MBC can be used as a safeguard to ensure that patients are not worsening, regardless of the clinician's assessment. A brief questionnaire administered at every treatment session is a relatively inexpensive way of achieving this safeguard.

I found the section of the article describing the barriers to MBC to be particularly helpful. Indeed, the main reason why less than 20% of providers are using MBC is that many settings do not have electronic health record systems that allow for automated scoring and graphing of MBC data, or do not have the training to interpret the MBC data. Additionally, many payers are not willing to reimburse for MBC. The authors are correct in stating that a professional practice guideline, while it would not address these concerns, would at least help to change the default from not using MBC. The Joint Commission's standard on MBC would likely already have this effect for accredited behavioral health organizations.

I was somewhat taken aback by the arguments in the section on legal or regulatory issues. The authors seem to be arguing that a professional practice guideline for MBC is needed in order for psychologists to be at the table when payers are deciding on outcome measures. This is not a bad goal, but it would have been better to come out and say that rather than making an argument based on patient benefit. Nonetheless, I was convinced by the results on equity, such as the findings that Medicaid patients and patients with language and literacy barriers are less likely to have access to MBC when it is available.
