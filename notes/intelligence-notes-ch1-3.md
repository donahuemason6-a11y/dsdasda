# Detailed Notes — Intelligence: Chapters 1–3

Notes on Mark Lowenthal, *Intelligence: From Secrets to Policy* (Chapters 1–3): the nature of intelligence, the development of U.S. intelligence, and the structure of the U.S. intelligence community.

---

## Chapter 1 — What Is Intelligence? (key closing points)

### Truth vs. proximate reality
- **Intelligence is not "truth."** Truth is an absolute standard that intelligence can rarely achieve; a better framing is **proximate reality** — the best, firmest possible understanding of what is going on.
- Goals of intelligence products: **reliable, unbiased, and honest** (free from politicization). These are laudable but are *different from truth*.
- The CIA lobby inscription ("And ye shall know the truth, and the truth shall make you free," John 8:32) is a nice sentiment but **overstates and misrepresents** what intelligence agencies actually do.

### Secrets vs. mysteries
- **Secrets:** information that someone, somewhere knows — just not us. The target of intelligence; the goal is to gain access to it.
- **Mysteries:** things that cannot be explained and for which no reliable intelligence likely exists (e.g., who built Stonehenge). Not a proper intelligence target.

### Is intelligence integral to the policy process?
- Yes, at one level: it provides **warning of imminent strategic threats**, seasoned advice, and information that would not exist without secret collection.
- Irony: agencies stress the **value added by analysis** over mere collection, although equally competent analysts exist in policy agencies — the difference is the nature of the work and the outcomes each is responsible for (intelligence vs. policy decisions).

### Four potential weaknesses of intelligence
1. **Conventional wisdom:** some analysis may be no more sophisticated than current conventional wisdom, which policy makers (partly justifiably) expect to be surpassed.
2. **Data dependence / missing intangibles:** analysis can miss factors that data cannot capture. Example: a competent 1770s political-military analysis would have concluded the thirteen colonies must lose to Britain (industrial power, trained troops, disunited colonial opinion, Native American allies) — missing intangibles like British divisiveness and possible French help.
3. **Mirror imaging:** assuming other states or individuals will act the way we would. Example: no U.S. policy maker in 1941 could conceive of Japan starting a war with the U.S. given the economic disparity — but in Tokyo that same disparity argued for starting war *sooner*. Mirror imaging also assumes shared rationality, leaving no room for the "irrational actor" (e.g., suicidal terrorists viewed through Western eyes).
4. **Policy makers can ignore intelligence:** they are free to reject or dispense with it at will; intelligence officers cannot force their products back into the process.

### Did intelligence matter? (the test)
- Retrospective test: **Would policy makers have made different choices with or without a given piece of intelligence?** If yes (or maybe), it mattered.
- **Richard Kerr** (former deputy DCI) reviewed fifty years of CIA analysis: despite highs and lows, intelligence consistently **reduced policy makers' uncertainty** and provided understanding and warning — a valuable service even though intelligence will not always be correct.

### Working concept and definitions
- **Working concept:** Intelligence is (1) the *process* by which specific types of information important to national security are requested, collected, analyzed, and provided to policy makers; (2) the *products* of that process; (3) the *safeguarding* of these processes and information by counterintelligence; and (4) the *carrying out of operations* as requested by lawful authorities.
- **Alan Breakspear** (Canadian intelligence veteran): intelligence is a capability to **forecast change — positive or negative — in time to do something about it**. The "positive" side is akin to **opportunity analysis**.
- Three simultaneous ways to think about intelligence:
  - **Process** — how information is required, requested, collected, analyzed, disseminated; how covert action is conceived and conducted.
  - **Product** — the analyses and operations themselves.
  - **Organization** — the units that carry out the functions.
- Key terms: intelligence, mirror imaging, national intelligence, opportunity analysis, politicized intelligence.

## Chapter 2 — The Development of U.S. Intelligence

Each nation practices intelligence in nation-specific ways — even among the **Five Eyes** (Australia, Canada, New Zealand, UK, U.S.). The U.S. system remains the largest and most influential (as model, rival, or target).

- "**Intelligence community**" is apt: agencies whose work is related and sometimes combined, but which serve different needs/policy makers under various lines of authority. It grew out of evolving demands **without a master plan** — highly functional yet sometimes dysfunctional.
- **DCI Richard Helms** (1966–1973): if created from scratch, much the same community would likely emerge — the focus is the *services* it provides, not the structure.
- The 2004 legislation creating the **DNI** changed the *superstructure*, not the essential functions of the agencies.

### Major themes

1. **Liberty vs. security.** Constant debate; not opposites, but at times one gives way to the other. Historical examples:
   - Alien and Sedition Acts (John Adams administration, 1797–1801)
   - Lincoln's suspensions of habeas corpus during the Civil War (Jefferson Davis did the same in the Confederacy)
   - Wilson's use of the Espionage Act in WWI; A. Mitchell Palmer's postwar raids on left-wing radicals
   - WWII internment of Japanese Americans (Nisei)
   - McCarthy hearings in the early Cold War
   - FBI/CIA investigation of antiwar and civil rights protesters under Johnson and Nixon
   - Post-9/11 concerns over NSA programs' breadth and domestic intrusiveness

2. **The novelty of U.S. intelligence.** The U.S. has the briefest significant intelligence history of the major powers (Sun Tzu, 5th c. BCE; Britain from Elizabeth I; France from Richelieu; Russia from Ivan the Terrible). First glimmer of a national enterprise only in 1940–41 with the **Coordinator of Information (COI, 1941)**, predecessor of the **OSS**. Reasons for the ~165-year absence:
   - No strong foreign policy interests beyond immediate borders; the **1823 Monroe Doctrine** (backed by tacit British support) solved basic security interests.
   - No serious threats from neighbors or outside powers (Civil War excepted).
   - Peacetime defense spending was severely limited until the Cold War.
   - Anomaly: intelligence took a remarkable **12% of the federal budget under George Washington** — never approached again. FY2024 request: **$101.7 billion ≈ 1.5%** of the total U.S. budget. Spending has grown in dollars since 9/11 but has stayed roughly stable as a *share* of the budget — the more important indicator.
   - Being a 1940s newcomer, intelligence lacked long-established patrons; it had rivals (Army, Navy, FBI) unwilling to share sources, and had to invent traditions under the pressures of WWII and the Cold War.

3. **A threat-based foreign policy.** After the Monroe Doctrine and especially the Spanish-American War (1898), the U.S. had a vested interest in the **status quo**. The twentieth century brought successive anti–status quo threats: Kaiserine Germany, the Axis, the Soviet Union. Responding to them became the mainstay of national security policy and gave focus to the operational side of intelligence (OSS → Cold War covert action).
   - **Terrorists** fit the anti–status quo pattern but are not nation-states; they *cannot* accept the status quo without giving up their raison d'être (unlike Nazi Germany or the USSR, which could temporarily accept it).
   - **China:** debated as a status quo threat — seeks great-power recognition and East Asian/western Pacific hegemony. Guarantor-of-status-quo costs: the Trump administrations appeared unwilling to bear them; the 2019 Worldwide Threat Assessment (DNI Coats) noted allies reassessing relations under "America first."

4. **Influence of the Cold War.** Would the U.S. have had large-scale intelligence without it? Author's view: **yes** — Pearl Harbor, not the Cold War, prompted formation. But the Cold War defined most basic forms and practices; it took **half the intelligence budget** (per DCI Robert Gates) until 1991, and the closed nature of the Soviet target forced reliance on **remote technical collection**.

5. **Global scope.** The Cold War went global (especially with decolonization); no region could be written off entirely, so the U.S. began collecting, analyzing, and stationing personnel everywhere.

6. **Wittingly redundant analytical structure.** Intelligence divides into four activities: **collection, analysis, covert action, counterintelligence**. Unique entities handle collection types and covert action; counterintelligence is everywhere. But for analysis, the U.S. *purposely* created three overlapping **all-source** agencies: **CIA Directorate of Analysis (DA)** (until 2015 the DI), **State/INR**, and **DIA**. Two reasons:
   - Different consumers have different needs; each policy agency wants a dedicated stream of analysis.
   - **Competitive analysis:** analysts with different backgrounds working the same issue counter parochial views and better approach proximate reality; in theory an antidote to **groupthink** (not always in practice — e.g., Iraq WMD, where inter-agency divisions over aluminum tubes etc. did not alter the predominant nuclear-capability view).
   - Cost: many analysts across agencies. 1990s "peace dividend" budget cuts (~**23,000 positions lost**, per DCI Tenet) forced "analytical triage" and less competitive analysis.

7. **Consumer–producer relations.** How close should intelligence be to policy?
   - **Distance school:** separation avoids politicization and policy-maker interference.
   - **Proximate school:** too much distance makes intelligence less aware of needs and less useful; training and reviews can prevent politicization.
   - The **proximate school won** by the late 1950s–early 1960s. Late-1990s shifts: heavy emphasis on support to military operations; analysts torn between operational and analytical customers.
   - Apotheosis under **George W. Bush**: PDB briefings six days a week, attended by DCI/DNI. Obama: read the PDB first, then a post-brief meeting with the DNI. Trump: less frequent, oral briefs. Biden: DNI and DCIA typically both attended — a regular president–DNI meeting has become standard.

8. **Analysis vs. collection/covert action (CIA structure).** DO handles espionage and covert action; the debate is whether housing DA and DO together risks objectivity (will analysts feel pressure to support ongoing covert action? e.g., contras in Nicaragua, 1980s) versus the benefits of proximity (analysts understand operations and source value; operators understand analysis). The **proximate school prevailed**: mid-1990s DI–DO partnership; post-Iraq-WMD effort to give analysts insight into DO sources (the **CURVEBALL** fiasco — a fabricating Iraqi source under German control whose recalled reporting was still used); **2015 Brennan reorganization** into regional/topical **mission centers** combining analytic and operational staffs (DA and DO now essentially logistic supports). Concern: homogenizing DA and DO cultures.

9. **Debate over covert action.** Persistent unease over propriety, especially **paramilitary operations** (contras, Mujahideen) — among the most controversial forms after assassination, with an uneven record. Debate intensity varies: little before the Bay of Pigs; revived in the 1970s (Vietnam-era collapse of the Cold War consensus + revelations of misdeeds) and the mid-1980s (contras); broad agreement post-9/11 (later debates over interrogation and **renditions** — nonjudicial apprehension of terrorists overseas). Newer issues: **armed UAV strikes** (including on U.S. citizens) and whether **cyber** preemption is military action or covert action.

10. **Continuity of intelligence policy.** For most of the Cold War, no Democratic–Republican difference; post-Vietnam differences were more rhetorical than real (Carter 1976 lumped CIA misdeeds with Watergate/Vietnam; Reagan 1980 spoke of "restoring" the CIA — but neither was simply anti- or pro-intelligence). Obama continued Bush-era counterterrorism substantially (Guantánamo stayed open as of 2025; UAV strikes *four times as often* as Bush; continued data programs; the 2011 CT strategy noted the continuity). The Worldwide Threat Assessment changed little from Clapper to Coats — until **Trump forbade further presentations to Congress** because it questioned his North Korea and Iran policy premises. Biden signaled a return to pre-Trump policy–intelligence relations.

11. **Heavy reliance on technology.** A U.S. hallmark since the 1940s (and in warfare since the Civil War); driven by the closed Soviet target. Persistent (unproven) perception that technical collection crowds out **HUMINT** — which can collect intentions and plans — though SIGINT sometimes can too. The underlying never-resolved issue: the proper **balance between technical and human collection** (raised again after 9/11).

12. **Secrecy vs. openness.** Democratic openness clashes with intelligence secrecy; no democracy has debated it more than the U.S. Ongoing series of compromises. Calls for "transparency" (UAVs, NSA programs) are the same debate reframed. **DNI Clapper released transparency principles (Oct 2015)** — insight into what intelligence does also builds domestic political support.

13. **Role of oversight.** First ~28 years: minimal congressional oversight (Cold War consensus; congressional abdication; secrecy). **After 1975**, oversight increased dramatically — Congress became a full participant and a major *consumer* (since 2002, even requesting NIEs). Open question: do the intelligence committees serve well as surrogates for the rest of Congress?

14. **Managing the community.** Size = breadth and depth, but also a coordination challenge. DCIs (1947–2004) were "**first among equals**," focused on the CIA (their source of bureaucratic clout). The DNI now has the community role minus the CIA — but every component except the CIA belongs to a **cabinet department**, diminishing the DNI's ability to give orders. Clapper's emphasis: "**intelligence integration**" (unity of purpose and effort), continued by Coats. The DNI can **lead but not command**.

### Major historical developments (post-1941)

| Event | Significance |
|---|---|
| **COI (1941) / OSS (1942)** | First steps toward a national capability (FDR; **William Donovan**, after trips to Britain). Heavily influenced by British practice — emphasis on covert action (Britain's only way to strike back at Germany early in the war). OSS covert actions had minimal effect on the war but became its main legacy; OSS was a training ground for the postwar community (though Helms notes most veterans' experience was espionage/CI, not covert action). Military distrust forced OSS under the Joint Chiefs — the start of enduring military–civilian intelligence tension (visible again in 2004 when DOD resisted expanding DNI authority). |
| **Pearl Harbor (1941)** | Classic intelligence failure: overlooked indicators, flawed processes, intelligence not shared across agencies, mirror imaging. Became the **guiding purpose** of the postwar community: prevent strategic surprise, especially in the nuclear-missile age. |
| **MAGIC and ULTRA (1941–45)** | MAGIC = U.S. intercepts of Japanese communications; ULTRA = British (later joint) intercepts of German communications. Demonstrated SIGINT's supreme wartime importance; solidified U.S.–British cooperation; the *military* (not OSS) controlled them — a precedent continued in **NSA**, a DOD **combat support agency** answerable to both the secretary of defense and the DNI. |
| **National Security Act (1947)** | Legal basis for the community, the **DCI**, and the **CIA**. Made intelligence permanent and civilian — a break from peacetime demobilization. CIA: not under military control; no domestic role or police powers; espionage/covert action/analysis not mentioned (vague "such other functions" language authorized clandestine activity). Truman's main concern: *coordination* of intelligence. Also created the secretary of defense and the **NSC**; the structure was stable for 57 years until the 2004 act. |
| **Korea (1950)** | Failure to foresee the invasion → DCI Walter Bedell Smith's reforms, incl. emphasis on **NIEs**; the Cold War became **global**, broadening intelligence scope. |
| **Iran coup (1953)** | U.S.-staged demonstrations overthrew Mossadegh, restored the shah. Success and ease made **covert action attractive** (esp. under DCI Allen Dulles / Eisenhower). |
| **Guatemala coup (1954)** | Overthrow of Arbenz (feared Soviet sympathy) via clandestine radio and air support. Proved Iran was not unique; further elevated covert action's appeal. |
| **Missile gap (1959–61)** | Post-Sputnik fear of a Soviet missile lead, pushed by Democratic hopefuls (Kennedy, Symington). Eisenhower knew from **U-2** reconnaissance it was untrue but stayed silent to protect sources. The legend that the intelligence community overestimated is **wrong**: agencies were divided (Air Force high), the public overestimate came from *political critics*, and the community actually **underestimated** Soviet MRBMs/IRBMs aimed at Europe. McNamara's distrust of Air Force parochialism → creation of **DIA**. Also an early case of intelligence as a political issue and of the costs of secrecy (Eisenhower couldn't reveal what he knew; U-2 flights ended after **Francis Gary Powers** was shot down over Sverdlovsk, May 1960). |
| **Bay of Pigs (1961)** | CIA-trained Cuban exile invasion failed abysmally (Kennedy limited it to preserve deniability). Showed the **limits of large-scale paramilitary operations** and of masking the U.S. role. DCI Dulles and other leaders retired; so were the Joint Chiefs as terms expired. |
| **Cuban Missile Crisis (1962)** | Initially an *analytic* failure — all analysts except **DCI John McCone** said Khrushchev wouldn't put missiles in Cuba; analysts also wrongly assumed no tactical nukes and no local launch authority (not known until 1992). A *collection* success: sites found before completion, giving Kennedy time; firm assessments aided by spy **Col. Oleg Penkovsky**. Model of collection disciplines working together; rehabilitated the community's post–Bay of Pigs reputation. |
| **Vietnam War (1964–75)** | (1) Politicization concerns — e.g., **Tet 1968**: intelligence warned of the offensive; Johnson chose to ride it out; military victory became political defeat; many wrongly assumed surprise. (2) **Order of battle debate**: CIA counted more enemy units than the military would accept; showed intelligence being used (mainly by the military) to portray progress. (3) Most important: the war **undercut the Cold War consensus** under which intelligence operated. |
| **ABM Treaty / SALT I (1972)** | Legitimized **national technical means (NTM)** and banned interference with them. Created **verification** (policy judgments about treaty compliance) vs. **monitoring** (tracking activities). Intelligence became central to arms-control politics, with politicization charges from both sides. |
| **Intelligence investigations (1975–76)** | After 1974 revelations of CIA domestic spying: **Rockefeller Commission** (found violations of law) and deeper House/Senate special committee investigations. Post-Watergate, post-Vietnam, they undermined faith in the community; intelligence never regained its former latitude. Congress created **permanent intelligence oversight committees** in both chambers. |
| **Iran (1979)** | Khomeini's revolution ousted the shah; U.S. intelligence largely blind — partly due to *policy decisions* limiting collection (no contacts with opposition lest the shah be offended), partly analytic failure to grasp the threat. Ramification: loss of two northern Iran collection sites used to monitor Soviet missile tests (impairing SALT monitoring). |
| **Iran-contra (1985–87)** | Reagan NSC staff used Iran missile-sale proceeds (against policy and law) to fund the contras despite congressional restrictions. Constitutional crisis; showed limits of oversight, executive ability to ignore congressional intent, and the danger of intertwining two covert actions. Undid much of Reagan's rebuilding of intelligence. |
| **Fall of the Soviet Union (1989–91)** | Triumph of **containment** (Kennan, 1946–47), but so swift few anticipated it. Critics: the ultimate intelligence failure; defenders: the community had reported the inner rot. Post–Cold War studies proposed changes, but few materialized until after 9/11 and the Iraq WMD estimate. |
| **Ames (1994) and Hanssen (2001)** | Ames (CIA): ~10 years spying for the USSR/Russia; exposed personnel, counterespionage, and CIA–FBI liaison failures (he was a marginal officer with a known alcohol problem). Earlier "**year of the spy**" (1985): Walker family (Navy comms), Pelton (NSA), Larry Wu-tai Chin (Chinese sleeper in CIA), Pollard (Israel). Hanssen (FBI): spied about as long, undetected longer — due to FBI laxness more than tradecraft; forced revision of the Ames damage assessment; a black eye for the FBI (which had focused wrongly on CIA officer Brian Kelley). Both cases proved great-power espionage survived the Cold War. |
| **9/11 and the war on terrorists (2001– )** | Attacks not anticipated in their specific form (bin Laden's enmity/capabilities known). Bush kept Tenet. Broad support for a full range of actions; **USA PATRIOT Act (2001)** expanded domestic collection and intel–law enforcement coordination; 2004 restructuring legislation followed. New collection developments (UAVs, real-time combat support); expanded CIA/NSA authorities (renditions; NSA metadata programs, leaked 2013). Two investigations found flaws but **no tactical intelligence** existed that could have precisely revealed the plot. Post-2009 shift to smaller individual attacks; the **May 2011 bin Laden operation** (multi-INT, years of analysis, sharing) restored confidence. Strains by 2013: UAV debates, NSA revelations, concern the CIA had become too tactical/militarized; the rise and rollback of **ISIS** and the returning-fighter problem. |
| **Iraq WMD (2003–08)** | Consensus that Saddam had WMD proved wrong; the two issues were how the intelligence erred and how policy makers used it. **Senate Intelligence Committee** found **groupthink** and unexamined premises but **no politicization**; the **WMD Commission** agreed on politicization but criticized collection and analysis broadly. The failure burdened U.S. intelligence for years; later, *unclassified* intelligence (e.g., the 2007 Iraq NIE key judgments, the 2007 Iran nuclear NIE reversal, the 2013 Syria CW debate) fueled political battles — politicization by *users* of intelligence rather than its writers. |
| **Reorganization (2004–05)** | Drivers: 9/11, the **9/11 Commission** report, missing Iraq WMD. **IRTPA** replaced the DCI with a **DNI** detached from any agency — the first major restructuring since 1947. The **WMD Commission** (Mar 2005) recommended further changes. Early instability: four DNIs in five-plus years (Negroponte, McConnell, Blair, Clapper); hard-to-fill senior jobs; Clapper then provided 6.5 years of stability. Trump-era instability returned (two confirmed + two acting DNIs in four years). |
| **Manning and Snowden leaks** | Manning (2010): ~700,000 documents to WikiLeaks (largely diplomatic cables); convicted under the Espionage Act, 35-year sentence commuted by Obama (released May 2017). Snowden (2013): NSA metadata programs plus much unrelated classified material — arguably the **worst leaks in U.S. history**; asylum then citizenship application in Russia. Issues raised: access controls, adequacy of laws, the internal-sharing ethos, diplomatic/capability damage, NSA authorities and oversight. Obama defended the programs as lawful (Jan 2014) yet prosecuted **ten leakers** — the most of any administration; press-freedom questions follow. |
| **Russian hacking and the 2016 election** | Jan 2017 CIA/FBI/NSA assessment: **Putin ordered an influence campaign** favoring Trump (no judgment on effect). Trump disputed it as delegitimizing. **Mueller** found extensive Trump campaign–Russia contacts, "sweeping and systematic" interference, but insufficient basis for collusion. **Durham** (2023) faulted FBI/DOJ investigations but found no political bias. The bipartisan **Senate Intelligence Committee** five-volume report defended the January 2017 assessment's analytic integrity; found contacts that "enabled" the Russian "assault on the integrity" of the election, but no collusion charge. 2020 cycle: ODNI analytic ombudsman accused Trump appointees of politicizing reporting; March 2021 assessment — Russia backed Trump's reelection, Iran worked against him, no votes changed. Helsinki 2018 (Trump accepting Putin's denials) and dismissal of the CIA's Khashoggi finding deepened the rift. Obama's 2016 restraint (fear of appearing to intervene for Clinton) was criticized by the Senate investigation. |
| **Domestic extremism (2021– )** | After the Jan 6 Capitol insurrection, **DNI Haines** directed investigation of domestic extremism (within the DNI's charter covering domestic and homeland intelligence). Limits: participants are mostly U.S. citizens, so CIA/NSA cannot take part except on **foreign connections** — legal "lanes in the road" must be observed. |
| **Russo-Ukraine War (2022– )** | Biden administration's aggressive **sharing of intelligence** to warn NATO and deter Putin — "intelligence diplomacy"; DCIA **William Burns** called it "**strategic declassification**." Accuracy of the shared intelligence sped NATO/EU consensus on supporting Ukraine. |

### The legal framework of intelligence
- **Constitution:** congressional power to create agencies, power of the purse, oversight basis; president as commander-in-chief; judiciary rules on constitutionality. **Bill of Rights** constraints: 1st (speech/press), 4th (search and seizure/warrants), 5th (due process), 8th (cruel and unusual punishment).
- **Espionage Act (1917):** wartime statute now the main basis for prosecuting leaks.
- **National Security Act (1947):** NSC, secretary of defense, JCS, DCI, CIA (vague charter; no police/subpoena power; "perform such other functions").
- **S. Res. 400 (1976):** charter of the Senate Select Committee on Intelligence.
- **FISA (1978):** procedures/warrants for foreign-intelligence surveillance; created the **FISC**; limited warrantless conditions.
- **Intelligence Oversight Act (1980):** Congress kept "**fully and currently informed**," including "any significant anticipated activity."
- **Classified Information Procedures Act (1980):** blocks "**graymail**" — judges review classified material without divulging it to juries.
- **Intelligence Identities Protection Act (1982):** federal crime to intentionally reveal a covert agent's identity.
- **USA PATRIOT Act (2001):** enhanced surveillance (citizens and noncitizens), roving wiretaps, improved sharing; extended/revised several times.
- **IRTPA (2004):** created the DNI over "national intelligence" (foreign + domestic + homeland); DNI separate from any agency; CIA head redesignated **DCIA**.
- **USA FREEDOM Act (2015):** ended Sec. 215 bulk collection; publication (with redactions) of significant FISC decisions.
- **EO 12333 (1981; amended 2004, 2008):** roles, responsibilities, and conduct rules for U.S. intelligence, protecting civil liberties.
- **EO 13526 (2009):** classification, safeguarding, declassification of national security information.
- **Intelligence Community Directives (ICDs)** issued by the DNI — notable ones: 107 (civil liberties/privacy/transparency), 112 (congressional notification), 116 (planning/programming/budgeting), 120 (whistleblower protection), **203 (analytic standards)**, 204 (National Intelligence Priorities Framework), 304 (HUMINT), 403 (foreign disclosure), 700/701/703 (protection of intelligence and classified information).

## Chapter 3 — The U.S. Intelligence Community

### The 2004 restructuring (IRTPA)
- Basic structure was remarkably stable from 1947 until post-9/11. The **9/11 Commission** (2004) recommendations — aided by savvy PR from the commission, staff, and September 11 families — were enacted after brief debate and intense bargaining.
- **Key change:** the **DNI** supplanted the DCI as senior intelligence official, head of the community, and principal intelligence adviser to the president and NSC.
- IRTPA redefined intelligence: no more foreign/domestic split; now one category — **national intelligence** — with three subsets: **foreign, domestic, homeland security**. Motive: agencies failed to share, especially across the foreign–domestic divide. The DNI must have access to all intelligence and ensure dissemination; also legally responsible for protecting **sources and methods**.
- The DNI heads no agency but oversees all, via a large staff (**ODNI**, under 2,000 in 2023 incl. NCTC; ~40% on rotation from other agencies). The DNI controls: **NCTC**, **National Counterproliferation and Biosecurity Center (NCPC)**, **National Intelligence Council (NIC)**, **NCSC**, **CTIIC**, and the **Foreign Malign Influence Center** (stood up 2022).
- DNI turnover: four DNIs 2005–2010 (instability); **Clapper** (2010–early 2017) longer than all predecessors combined; renewed instability under Trump (two confirmed, two acting, over four years — driven largely by Trump's desire for more supportive intelligence). Haines (2021–2025), first woman DNI; current DNI **Tulsi Gabbard (2025– )**.
- Military-influence concerns at Clapper's nomination (predecessors McConnell and Blair were retired flag officers; first principal deputy Hayden). Old rule (1996–2005, DCI + two deputies era): only one of the three could be a military officer (active or retired within ten years).
- Persistent core problem: **disparity between the DNI's responsibilities and authority** (inherited from the DCI era).
- "How would one know reform is working?" — vague answer (procedural/cultural change, better sharing) underscores the IRTPA's conceptual weakness: broad, previously identified findings produced a grandiose but amorphous response. Clapper: integration of collection and analysis; Coats continued; Haines's 2023 National Intelligence Strategy had six goals but no overarching strategic objective.

### Budget-sector view (Figure 3.1)
- **NIP** (National Intelligence Program; formerly NFIP, renamed to include homeland/domestic) — managed by the **DNI**.
- **MIP** (Military Intelligence Program) — managed by the **secretary of defense**; merged the former JMIP and TIARA.
- The secretary of defense controls **75–80%** of the community day-to-day (NSA, DIA, NGA, NRO, airborne reconnaissance, five service intelligence units, intelligence components of the eleven combatant commands). Much defense intelligence responsibility is delegated to the **USDI&S** (under secretary of defense for intelligence and security; created 2002 as USDI).
- Budget authority fight: the DNI develops/determines the NIP from agency submissions and gives guidance, but lacks **budget execution authority**. The DNI may transfer/reprogram up to **$150 million or 5%** of any agency's NIP funds (for higher priority/emergent needs; not to terminate an acquisition program). DOD kept budget control of NSA, NGA, NRO.

### Functional view (Figure 3.2)
- Two broad areas: **management** (requirements, resources, collection, production) and **execution** (systems development; collection/analysis/production; operations; infrastructure).
- **Evaluation** straddles both — historically one of the community's weakest functions (improved under Clapper via the **Systems and Research Analyses (SRA)** office).
- If the circular flow starts anywhere, it is **requirements** — they should drive everything. The **National Intelligence Priorities Framework (NIPF)** (created 2003 under Tenet, now in ODNI; priorities set by NSC members) is the main requirements driver and also a performance-assessment tool. Clapper's integration push created **national intelligence managers (NIMs)** responsible for **unifying intelligence strategies (UIS)**.
- Collection and systems development occupy far more of the picture than analysis — reflecting community realities, desirable or not.

### The many communities (Figure 3.3 — functions view)
- "The simplicity of intelligence" (Bull Durham analogy): *you ask a question; you collect information; you answer the question* — the devils are in the details.
- **Top level (clients/managers):** president (client, not manager); secretaries of defense, state, treasury, commerce, energy, attorney general (clients; defense, state, and the AG control significant assets). DHS (2002) has two IC components: **Coast Guard intelligence** and the **Office of Intelligence and Analysis (I&A)**. FBI has a **National Security Branch** (intelligence + CI + counterterrorism + WMD office). Treasury's **Office of Terrorism and Financial Intelligence** grows in importance (sanctions). Energy: small office + national labs coordination. Commerce: commercial attachés (overt collection). DEA under the AG.
- **JICC** (Joint Intelligence Community Council, created by IRTPA) — largely bypassed in favor of the **EXCOM** (DNI + agency heads + senior policy makers; there is also a DEXCOM). A similar EXCOM existed under DCI Gates.
- **Builders:** **NRO** (designs/builds/launches satellites via contractors); DOD airborne ("air-breathing") systems incl. UAVs; CIA **DS&T**.
- **Collectors:** NSA (**SIGINT**); NGA (**IMINT/GEOINT** processing and exploitation); DOD airborne systems; Defense Attaché System (overt HUMINT) and DIA's **Defense Clandestine Service** (clandestine HUMINT); CIA **DO** (espionage/HUMINT; also houses the **Open Source Enterprise**); State (via embassies/FSOs — mostly not "tasked" collection); Commerce (attachés); FBI (CI collection; legal attachés overseas). The DNI has authority to manage and task collection.
- **All-source producers:** CIA/**DA** (largest), DIA/DI, State/**INR**; plus the five service intelligence offices; FBI's Intelligence Directorate (codified 2004 — a culture clash for a law-enforcement-first agency struggling to integrate non-agent analysts); DHS I&A and Coast Guard; Energy. DNI-controlled analytic bodies: **NIC** (NIOs; produces **NIEs**), **NCTC** (all terrorism analysis except purely domestic), **NCPC** (coordinates WMD-proliferation intelligence planning; fills gaps; does **not** produce intelligence per se), **CTIIC** (est. 2015 at Obama White House's behest despite IC pushback; integrates all-source foreign cyber-threat intelligence), Foreign Malign Influence Center.
- **IRTPA analytic mandates** (reflecting Iraq WMD more than 9/11): (1) a process for **alternative analysis**; (2) an official/office responsible for **analytic integrity** (timeliness, objectivity, all-source use, tradecraft); (3) an appointee to oversee/report on objectivity and tradecraft quality. 2007: analytic-quality evaluation standards released.
- **Counterintelligence:** NCSC under the DNI; FBI National Security Branch coordinates foreign CI in the U.S.; CIA/DO has its own CI/counterespionage components; DIRNSA is also director of the **Central Security Service** (safeguarding U.S. communications).
- **Centers substructure:** cross-agency (DNI centers) and within-agency. CIA: all issues now handled by **mission centers** (formerly CIC, CNC, CTC). DIA hosts: National Center for Medical Intelligence, Missile and Space Intelligence Center, National Media Exploitation Center, Underground Facilities Analysis Center, JITF/CT, POW-MIA Analytic Cell. Service centers: Marine Corps Intelligence Activity, National Air and Space Intelligence Center, National Maritime Intelligence Center, National Ground Intelligence Center, plus the Space Force's National Space Intelligence Center. Flexibility, but fragmentation/overlap/sharing concerns.
- **Space Force** (Dec 2019): the **eighteenth and newest** IC member. NRO control in wartime long disputed; agreement: NRO will "**take direction**" from the Space Force commander in wartime and execute defensive operations (details uncertain); joint command at the National Space Defense Center, Colorado Springs. NRO remains a Defense agency jointly overseen by DNI and SecDef; merger with Space Force resisted.

### Relationships that matter
Wiring diagrams deceive: they show position, not interaction — and **personalities matter**.

**DNI–president.** Crucial; the DNI embodies the community. Helms: the DCI's authority derived from *perceived access* to the president. Cautionary cases: McCone (excluded by Johnson over Vietnam, resigned), Woolsey (little access to Clinton). Both DNI and DCIA now attend NSC meetings — effectively **two senior intelligence officials** at the table. Trump's early NSC structure conditioned DNI/CJCS attendance (while Bannon attended regularly — later reversed; Bannon fired Aug 2017). Trump-era churn: **Coats** fired essentially over his 2019 Worldwide Threat Assessment testimony (North Korea won't denuclearize; Iran largely compliant); deputy **Sue Gordon** passed over and resigned; **Ratcliffe** withdrew (thin credentials, embellished résumé), later confirmed on a party-line vote (ombudsman charged he politicized intelligence); acting DNI **Maguire** fired for briefings that Russia favored Trump in 2020; acting DNI **Grenell** (no intelligence experience) forced out career officers, reorganized NCTC, refused election-security testimony, declassified Flynn case documents.
- Too close risks objectivity (Tenet–Bush and Iraq WMD — though the SSCI found no politicization); if forced to choose, an **overly close relationship beats a distant one**.
- No fixed term for the DNI (unlike the FBI's 10-year term); serves at the president's pleasure. Fixed-term pros: continuity/objectivity across administrations; cons: a president stuck with a DNI without rapport → diminished access (see also Freeh–Clinton/Reno, Comey's firing). Partisan turnover of DCIs began with Carter declining to retain George H. W. Bush (1977), practice until Bush kept Tenet (2001).
- IRTPA requires only vague "extensive national security experience." Few DCIs were professional intelligence officers (Helms, Colby, Gates, partly Goss); DCIAs with significant experience: Hayden, Morell (acting twice), Brennan, Haspel. Negroponte set the precedent that the DNI need not be a career intelligence officer — career policy makers can succeed because they know what support policy makers need.
- The DNI controls few analysts (essentially NIC + NCTC) → weak institutional base; agency heads are rivals with more control and insight; the DNI is senior adviser while relying on others' analysis (especially the CIA's).

**DNI–CIA.** The CIA lost status with the DNI's creation but kept the key roles: **all-source analysis, HUMINT, intelligence operations, foreign liaison**. Former DCIs Webster and Tenet argued a DNI without control of these could not be effective. **Blair–Panetta dispute**: Blair claimed authority to name **chiefs of station (COS)** (technically the DNI's representatives) and wanted more covert-action oversight; NSA James Jones sided with the CIA (DNI got authority to evaluate covert-action effectiveness when the White House asks); Blair resigned with little administration support. **ICD 402** ("DNI Representatives," 2012): COSs act as DNI representatives; the DNI can raise performance issues and recommend removal, but the **DCIA controls COSs**. Brennan as Obama's counterterrorism adviser out-accessed the DNI; becoming DCIA (2013) ended the White House rivalry but created a DCIA with a pre-established close presidential relationship.
- **PDB:** under DCIs a CIA product; now a **DNI product, ~75% CIA content**. Presentation morphs per president (Bush: single meeting with DCI/DNI present; Obama: read first, then meeting; Trump: less frequent, oral, reportedly paused in 2020; Biden: daily schedule restored).
- CIA officers traditionally support the NSC's **Principals Committee** and **Deputies Committee**; the DNI is the intelligence participant at PCs (DCIA also attends), relying mainly on **NIOs** — coordination/briefing-book work that arguably distracts the NIC from senior analytic expertise.
- Posner's frame: DNI as **CEO** (community level) vs. **COO** (details). Daily demands (PDB, PCs/DCs, Congress) force COO behavior; Clapper tried to be CEO. Either way, the DNI is held responsible for overall performance.
- DNI–NCTC director: NCTC head is semiautonomous (presidentially appointed, Senate-confirmed, principal adviser on terrorism analysis/operations) and **reports directly to the president** on joint counterterrorism operational planning — a built-in rivalry potential.

**DNI–State / ambassadors.** Intelligence is widely seen as an arm of foreign policy. Issues: coordinating operations with policy goals; **State cover** for clandestine officers. Dulles brothers' warmth (Allen at CIA, John Foster at State) was the exception; the relationship usually has an edge. Overseas: long tradition of tension between **ambassadors** (who head the whole country team) and **COSs**, who have not always kept ambassadors informed. Biden-era declassification diplomacy requires close State–intelligence cooperation.

**DNI–Defense.** Not a relationship of equals: SecDef controls most of the community and most of the client base; the intelligence budget hides within the defense budget (a subterfuge continued largely at Congress's behest even after 2010, when Gates and Clapper began declassifying NIP and MIP totals; current-year requests now declassified too). Negroponte faced aggressive Rumsfeld/Cambone initiatives seemingly aimed at separate DOD capabilities; Gates as SecDef (a former DCI) scaled these back and helped select Clapper as DNI. The **USDI&S** functions as COO of defense intelligence; the key is ODNI credibility with OSD — if OSD thinks DOD needs are neglected, it can stymie the DNI.

**DHS.** Expected to be a second "800-pound gorilla" but never became one — too large and unwieldy (22+ agencies merged in 2002). Only Coast Guard and I&A are in the IC/NIP; many components with intelligence functions are not (Secret Service, CBP, ICE, TSA, CISA). DHS coordinates terrorist-information sharing across federal/state/local/tribal levels — **JTTFs** (federal, ~200 cities) and **79 fusion centers** — criticized as too large and duplicative; I&A criticized for little value-added analysis (acknowledged by Under Secretary Francis X. Taylor). Trump appointees allegedly buried right-wing domestic terrorism reporting; Mayorkas created an I&A domestic terrorism branch and a Homeland Intelligence Experts Group; 2023 reports on I&A's **Overt Human Intelligence Collection Program** (interviews of detainees/prisoners, monitoring public views) → several activities prohibited by the FY2024 NDAA.

**DNI–national security adviser.** The NSA (not Senate-confirmed) controls paper/briefing flow to the president; Kissinger and Brzezinski personally delivered the PDB and limited DCI access. A national security adviser who blocks access is a serious problem for the DNI.

**DNI–Congress.** Three components: (1) **power of the purse** — Congress funds and shapes programs, sometimes champions programs the executive opposes; (2) **personal relations** — DCIs who fought their overseers lost (Casey's contempt, Woolsey vs. DeConcini, Deutch vs. HPSCI); Congress watches whether the DNI meets the expectations it legislated; (3) **public perception** — citizens glimpse intelligence mainly through congressional activity; investigations signal trouble; legislators are surrogates for the public. Hayden's point: U.S. intelligence operates **by permission of Congress** — sustaining that permission requires transparency and supportive legislators. Russia-investigation era: unprecedented (mostly Republican) demands for underlying materials strained inter-branch relations even under unified party control.

**USDI(&S) and defense agencies.** Created 2002 for Rumsfeld (one funnel for defense intelligence). October 2014 directive broadened the portfolio (cybersecurity, insider threats, unauthorized disclosures, biometrics); title changed to USDI&S; Defense Counterintelligence and Security Agency added. Small staff; pure management oversight (no line assets) yet highly influential on policy, requirements, budgets. NSA/NGA heads serve **two masters** (DOD and DNI); to clarify hierarchy on the EXCOM, the USDI&S was designated **director of defense intelligence** under the DNI — a function with **no legal status**, suspendable at will.
- **CCMDs** (combatant commands): rivalry over collection assets; regional CCMDs demand collection Washington may not support. Each CCMD now has a **JIOC** (Joint Intelligence Operations Center) reflecting tight operations–intelligence integration (e.g., the 2003 "thunder run" into Baghdad).
- USDI&S is one of two conduits of defense intelligence issues to Congress (the other: DIA), dealing with the Armed Services Committees and guarding the SecDef's authority against encroachment.

**INR–State seventh floor.** Smallest all-source producer, sometimes seen as weakest. Its clout depends on access to the secretary and senior officials ("the seventh floor") — highly variable (Shultz met all assistant secretaries regularly; Baker only a few seniors, so INR's clients were mostly other bureaus). INR works to involve bureaus in requirements to build appreciation and support.

**Areas of rivalry.** The war on terrorists intensified rivalry by (1) blurring foreign/domestic and intelligence/military-operations lines (terrorists inside the U.S. legally; CIA-led liaison with the Northern Alliance blending covert, conventional, and special-operations roles — all visible in the 2011 bin Laden raid), and (2) organizations' natural wartime expansion.
- **FBI vs. CIA:** FBI expanded legal attachés abroad (sometimes allegedly without telling the CIA); fought over recruiting foreigners in the U.S. for overseas collection (CIA allowed to run it since collection is abroad; FBI claimed it as domestic); concerns about duplication/cross-purposes and FBI inexperience. 2012: Clapper made FBI **SACs** in certain cities DNI representatives — domestic equivalents of COSs.
- **DOD vs. CIA:** Rumsfeld's frustration at CIA speed into Afghanistan and dependence on CIA HUMINT; FY2005 $25M to **SOCOM** for supporting foreign forces (covert action without findings/reporting apparatus? possible "double dipping" by paid foreigners); WMD Commission recommended greater DOD covert-action authority — Bush administration declined (2005); DO now has an assistant director coordinating all clandestine overseas HUMINT community-wide. 2012 SOCOM bid to train/equip internal security forces (Yemen, Kenya) rejected after State/congressional objections; 2014 Congress rejected shifting UAV programs from CIA to Defense. DIA's Strategic Support Branch (HUMINT augmentation) raised oversight/duplication questions; Gates scaled such initiatives back. **DCS** (2012, Clapper/Panetta): expanded defense HUMINT, trained with the DO; CIA reportedly favored it, but SASC barred its personnel expansion (Dec 2012) and HASC withheld half its funding (May 2013) pending proof of uniqueness.
- **Domestic extremism** spreads responsibility: FBI leads domestically; CIA/NSA handle foreign linkages; DOD looks within its own ranks.

**Congressional relationships.**
- **Senate:** SSCI has sole jurisdiction over only the **DNI, CIA, NIC**; SASC jealously guards defense intelligence — relations standoffish-to-hostile (SASC punishes perceived turf intrusions, e.g., delaying authorization bills). The 2004 reorganization bill went to the Governmental Affairs Committee — seen as a slap at SSCI chair Pat Roberts, whose more radical proposal predated it.
- **House:** HPSCI has exclusive jurisdiction over the entire **NIP** and shared jurisdiction with HASC over military programs — a better working relationship than the Senate pair (though HASC was DOD's strongest advocate in 2004–05).
- Authorizers vs. **appropriators** (Defense Appropriations subcommittees): appropriators resent authorizers; smoother in the House. From **2005 to 2010 no intelligence authorization bill became law**, weakening the committees (FY2010 bill signed after FY2011 began).
- SSCI's unique power: **confirmation** of the DNI, principal deputy, some subordinates, and the DCIA.
- Russia-investigation contrast: Senate (Burr/Warner) collegial, supported the ICA; the House committee ceased functioning coherently amid extreme partisanship (Republicans opposing, Democrats supporting the investigation); partial improvement after 2022 personnel changes, with lingering hostility toward Adam Schiff.

### The intelligence budget process
- "Money is the root of all government." Two components since simplification: **NIP** (transcends any agency or is nondefense; DNI responsible) and **MIP** (defense/service programs; SecDef responsible; combines old JMIP + TIARA).
- Old rule of thumb: NIP ≈ 2× MIP; the ratio has shifted further toward the NIP (MIP cut faster). Figures:
  - FY2010 peak: **$80.1B** ($53.1B NIP / $27B MIP)
  - FY2015 trough: **$66.8B**
  - FY2022 appropriated: **$89.8B** ($65.7B / $24.1B)
  - FY2023 appropriated: **$99.6B** ($71.7B / $27.9B)
  - FY2024 request: **$101.7B** ($72.4B / $29.3B)
- DNI power looks large (NIP responsibility) but is limited: **no budget execution authority** over NIP agencies.
- **NIP programs** include: CIA Program (CIAP); ODNI Community Management Account (CMA); CIA Retirement and Disability System (CIARDS); FBI counterintelligence; DHS program; State/INR; Treasury Office of Intelligence Support; Consolidated Cryptologic Program (CCP); General Defense Intelligence Program (GDIP); National Geospatial-Intelligence Program (NGP); National Reconnaissance Program (NRP).
- **MIP programs**: Air Force, Army, Navy, Marine Corps, Space Force intelligence; SOCOM; Defense Airborne Reconnaissance Program (DARP); Defense Cryptologic Program (DCP); Defense General Intelligence Applications Program (DGIAP); Defense Geospatial-Intelligence Program; Defense Intelligence Counterdrug Program (DICP); Defense Intelligence Special Technologies Program (DISTP); Defense Intelligence Tactical Program (DITP); Defense Space Reconnaissance Program (DSRP).
- **Fencing:** NIP funds are fenced — tradable only among NIP intelligence programs; MIP funds are **not** fenced and can go to non-intelligence needs. Most NIP money sits inside intelligence agencies; most MIP money sits in non-intelligence Defense entities (the services, SOCOM). NIP trade-offs are intelligence-vs-intelligence; MIP programs must defend against weapons/personnel priorities.
- **Process (four phases over ~three years):** Planning (guidance, Mar–Sep Year 1) → Programming (requests/review, multiyear) → Budgeting (build and submit) → Execution (obligate and spend). Executive build takes 13+ months (DNI guidance ~November → completed budget to the president the following December), including **crosswalks** with DOD (coordination and hard choices, up to DNI/SecDef level). The president's budget goes to Congress in February; ~8 months of hearings, markups, floor action, conference, final passage. "The president proposes and Congress disposes."
- **Supplementals:** appropriations above the regular budget; disliked by agencies (one-year money) but useful to Congress (no long-term commitment). **OCO** (overseas contingency operations) funded much Iraq/Afghanistan activity as a wartime supplemental; its decline hit CCMD intelligence especially hard.
- At any moment, up to **eight fiscal-year budgets** are simultaneously live: two past years still being spent, the current year, the next year in the political process, one being formulated, one nearing program completion, and two in long-range planning. Mastery of the process = influence.
