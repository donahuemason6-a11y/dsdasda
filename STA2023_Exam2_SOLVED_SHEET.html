Q1 (6 pts) — Two-way table: or / and / given

"The table below summarizes the employment status of 78 residents." Males / Females: Full-time 22 / 18 (row 40), Part-time 6 / 10 (16), Unemployed 3 / 2 (5), Not in labor force 5 / 12 (17); males 36, females 42, total 78.

First: total each row and column at the table's edge (if the counts come in words, draw the table yourself). Every answer is then a count ÷ the group you are picking from.

(a) probability that a randomly selected resident is male?
one category (a row or a column) → its total ÷ grand total (78)
\(P(\text{male})=\dfrac{36}{78}=0.4615\)
(b) …is part-time?
same rule: add the row's cells, ÷ grand total
\(P(\text{part-time})=\dfrac{6+10}{78}=\dfrac{16}{78}=0.2051\)
(c) …is male OR unemployed?
OR: \(P(A\text{ or }B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\text{ and }B)\) — adding counts the overlap (male AND unemployed = 3) twice, so take it out once
\(P(\text{M or U})=\dfrac{36}{78}+\dfrac{5}{78}-\dfrac{3}{78}=\dfrac{38}{78}=0.4872\)
Watch out: forgetting the overlap gives 41/78 ✗ (check: 36 males + 2 unemployed women = 38 ✓)
(d) …is female AND not in the labor force (NLF)?
AND in a table = the ONE cell where the row and column meet, ÷ grand total
\(P(\text{F and NLF})=\dfrac{12}{78}=0.1538\)
(e) …is part-time GIVEN that the resident is female?
GIVEN: \(P(B\mid A)=\dfrac{\#(A\text{ and }B)}{\#(A)}\) — the letter after | is the given group (the word after "given"): divide by its total, 42, not 78
\(P(\text{PT}\mid\text{Female})=\dfrac{10}{42}=0.2381\)
Watch out: 10/78 = 0.1282 is the classic error.

Q4 (8 pts) — Driving age: binomial

"According to a Gallup poll, 60% of U.S. women 18 years old or older stated that the minimum driving age should be 18. In a random sample of 10 U.S. women 18 years old or older, find the probability that:"

Binomial: n = the sample size (10 yes/no trials); p = the percent in the question as a decimal. n = 10, p = 0.60, 1 − p = 0.40, X = number who say yes.

\(P(x)={}_{n}C_{x}\;p^{x}\,(1-p)^{\,n-x}\qquad {}_{n}C_{x}=\dfrac{n!}{x!\,(n-x)!}\)
(a) exactly 3 believe the minimum driving age should be 18
\({}_{10}C_{3}=\dfrac{10!}{3!\,7!}=\dfrac{10\cdot 9\cdot 8}{3\cdot 2\cdot 1}=120\)
\(P(3)={}_{10}C_{3}\,(0.60)^{3}(0.40)^{7}=120(0.216)(0.0016384)=0.0425\)
0.0425
Watch out: the power on 0.40 is n − x = 7, not 3. ₁₀C₃: 10! = 10·9·8·7!, the 7! cancels → 10·9·8 ÷ (3·2·1), or press the nCr key.
(b) fewer than 3 believe…
"fewer than 3" = 0, 1, 2 — the 3 is OUT. Add the three (₁₀C₀ = 1, 0.6⁰ = 1); round each to 4 decimals, then add those.
\(P(0)={}_{10}C_{0}(0.6)^{0}(0.4)^{10}=1(1)(0.0001049)=0.0001\)
\(P(1)={}_{10}C_{1}(0.6)^{1}(0.4)^{9}=10(0.6)(0.0002621)=0.0016\)
\(P(2)={}_{10}C_{2}(0.6)^{2}(0.4)^{8}=45(0.36)(0.0006554)=0.0106\)
\(P(X<3)=0.0001+0.0016+0.0106=0.0123\)
0.0123
(c) between 1 and 3 (inclusive) believe…
inclusive → x = 1, 2, 3. Reuse (a) and (b) — write "from part (a)".
\(P(1\le X\le 3)=P(1)+P(2)+P(3)=0.0016+0.0106+0.0425=0.0547\)
0.0547
(d) sample of 200: expected number and standard deviation
binomial shortcuts: \(\mu=np,\qquad \sigma=\sqrt{np(1-p)}\)
\(\mu=np=200(0.60)=120\)
\(\sigma=\sqrt{np(1-p)}=\sqrt{200(0.60)(0.40)}=\sqrt{48}=6.93\)

CH 4 — PROBABILITY

  • Probability = the long-run fraction of times something happens (Law of Large Numbers: more trials → the fraction settles at the probability). 0 ≤ P(E) ≤ 1; all outcomes together add to 1. Unusual event = probability below 0.05.
  • Sample space S = everything that could happen; an event = any part of it.
  • Classical (equally likely outcomes — dice, cards, coins): count. \(P(E)=\dfrac{N(E)}{N(S)}\) P(two heads) = 1/4; P(at least one tail) = 3/4.
  • Empirical (from data): \(P(E)\approx\dfrac{\text{frequency of }E}{\text{number of trials}}\) 22 of 200 carpool → 0.11 (interpret: "if many people were selected, about 11 of every 100 would carpool"); 5 walk → 5/200 = 0.025 < 0.05 → unusual. Subjective = educated guess.
  • Disjoint (mutually exclusive) = can't both happen (one card: king AND queen), so P(E and F) = 0. Independent = E happening doesn't change P(F) (two coin flips). Disjoint is NOT independent: if they are disjoint, knowing E happened means F didn't.

The rules — spot the keyword

NOT: \(P(E^{c})=1-P(E)\)  ·  AT LEAST ONE: \(P(\text{at least 1})=1-P(\text{none})\)
OR: \(P(E\text{ or }F)=P(E)+P(F)-P(E\text{ and }F)\)  (disjoint → the overlap is 0, just add)
AND, independent: \(P(E\text{ and }F)=P(E)\cdot P(F)\)  ·  AND, always: \(P(E\text{ and }F)=P(E)\cdot P(F\mid E)\)
GIVEN: \(P(F\mid E)=\dfrac{P(E\text{ and }F)}{P(E)}=\dfrac{N(E\text{ and }F)}{N(E)}\)  ·  Independent? check \(P(F\mid E)=P(F)\): equal → independent, different → dependent

"At least one": 1 − P(none). P(survive) = 0.9986: P(both of 2 survive) = 0.9986² = 0.9972; P(at least one of 1000 dies) = 1 − 0.99861000 = 0.7536.

Counting — "how many ways"

  • A sequence of choices: multiply the options at each step. 3-letter codes: 26·26·26 = 17,576; chair, vice-chair, secretary from 14: 14·13·12 = 2,184.
  • Permutation = pick r of n, ORDER MATTERS (1st/2nd/3rd). \({}_{n}P_{r}=\dfrac{n!}{(n-r)!}\) ₇P₅ = 7·6·5·4·3 = 2,520 (r numbers counting down); ₁₀P₃ = 720; 7 in order: 7! = 5,040; note 0! = 1: ₅P₅ = 5! = 120; ₙC₀ = ₙCₙ = 1.
  • Combination = pick r of n, order DOESN'T matter (committee, poker hand, sample). \({}_{n}C_{r}=\dfrac{n!}{r!\,(n-r)!}\) ₄C₂ = 4!/(2!·2!) = 24/(2·2) = 6; ₂₀C₄ = 4,845. Probability by counting: P(4 cards all the same rank) = 13 ranks ÷ ₅₂C₄ = 13/270,725 ≈ 0.00005.

CH 5 — DISCRETE RANDOM VARIABLES ★Q2 ★Q3

  • Random variable X = the number that comes out of a chance experiment. Discrete = you count it; its possible values are a list (x = 0, 1, 2, …). Continuous = you measure it; its possible values are an interval (time t ≥ 0, mass 0 < m ≤ 16 oz). Probability distribution = every value X can take with its probability.
VALID only if (1) every \(0\le P(x)\le 1\) AND (2) \(\sum P(x)=1\)
\(\mu_X=E(X)=\sum\big[x\cdot P(x)\big]\)
\(\sigma_X=\sqrt{\sum\big[(x-\mu)^2\cdot P(x)\big]}\)

Mean of a distribution = weighted average (each value counts as much as its probability) = the long-run average per trial. SD = typical distance from the mean, each squared gap weighted by its probability.

Q: "Insurance: premium $100, pays $10,000 if the client dies, P(dies) = 0.0014. Expected profit?" A: lives → +$100 with P = 0.9986; dies → 100 − 10,000 = −$9,900 with P = 0.0014. E(X) = 100(0.9986) − 9900(0.0014) = 99.86 − 13.86 = $86 per policy → keep selling.

CH 6 — CONTINUOUS & NORMAL

  • A continuous variable has infinitely many values, so probability = AREA under the curve (pdf = the probability curve: never below 0, total area 1). P(X = one exact value) = 0 so P(a < X < b) = P(a ≤ X ≤ b).

Uniform ★Q5

Uniform from a to b = a flat rectangle: every stretch of the same length is equally likely. Probability of an interval = its share of the total width.

\(\text{height}=\dfrac{1}{b-a}\qquad P(c\le X\le d)=\dfrac{d-c}{b-a}\)
\(\text{value with share }P\text{ below it: } x=a+P\,(b-a)\)

Normal ★Q6 ★Q7

Normal = the bell curve: symmetric around μ, most values near the middle. μ slides it left/right, σ makes it wider or narrower. Properties: symmetric about μ; mean = median = mode = μ (the peak); bends (inflection points) at μ ± σ; total area 1; tails never touch the axis; Empirical Rule: 68% of values within 1σ of μ, 95% within 2σ, 99.7% within 3σ (pieces 34 | 13.5 | 2.35 | 0.15 per side); half the area on each side of μ.

Standard normal Z: μ = 0, σ = 1. The trick: convert any value to a z-score and use ONE table (or the exam's hint): it gives the area to the LEFT of z.

\(z=\dfrac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\ \ (\text{round to 2 decimals})\qquad x=\mu+z\sigma\)
LEFT ("less than / below"): table value  ·  RIGHT ("more than / above"): 1 − table value
BETWEEN: (left of the bigger z) − (left of the smaller z)  ·  no −z row in the table? left of −z = 1 − (left of +z)

Steps: draw the bell, mark μ and x, shade → z = (x − μ)/σ to 2 decimals → hint or table (z = 1.67: row 1.6, column .07) = area to the LEFT → left: done · right: 1 − it · between: big left − small left.
