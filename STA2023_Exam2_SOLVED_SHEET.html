Q1 (6 pts) — Two-way table: or / and / given

"The table below summarizes the employment status of 78 residents. Answer each question. Show your work."
StatusMalesFemalesRow total
Full-time221840
Part-time61016
Unemployed325
Not in labor force51217
Column total364278

First: add the row and column totals in the margins. Every answer is then a count ÷ the group you are picking from.

(a) probability that a randomly selected resident is male?
one category → its total ÷ grand total
\(P(\text{male})=\dfrac{36}{78}=0.4615\)
(b) …is part-time?
a row → add both cells, then ÷ 78
\(P(\text{part-time})=\dfrac{6+10}{78}=\dfrac{16}{78}=0.2051\)
(c) …is male OR unemployed?
OR: \(P(A\text{ or }B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\text{ and }B)\) — adding counts the overlap (male AND unemployed = 3) twice, so take it out once
\(P(\text{M or U})=\dfrac{36}{78}+\dfrac{5}{78}-\dfrac{3}{78}=\dfrac{38}{78}=0.4872\)
Watch out: forgetting the overlap gives 41/78 = 0.5256 ✗ (check: 36 males + 2 unemployed women = 38 ✓)
(d) …is female AND not in the labor force?
AND in a table = the ONE cell where the row and column meet, ÷ 78
\(P(\text{F and NLF})=\dfrac{12}{78}=0.1538\)
(e) …is part-time GIVEN that the resident is female?
GIVEN: \(P(B\mid A)=\dfrac{N(A\text{ and }B)}{N(A)}\) — A = the given event (the word after "given"): divide by that group's total, 42, not 78
\(P(\text{PT}\mid\text{Female})=\dfrac{10}{42}=0.2381\)
Watch out: 10/78 = 0.1282 is the classic error.
If they switch it up: NOT male → \(1-\dfrac{36}{78}=\dfrac{42}{78}=0.5385\)  ·  female GIVEN part-time → \(\dfrac{10}{16}=0.6250\) (direction matters)  ·  independent? P(PT | F) = 0.2381 ≠ P(PT) = 0.2051 → dependent

Q2 (2 pts) — Valid discrete probability distribution?

"Determine whether the following distribution represents a valid discrete probability distribution. If not, explain why." x: 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 with P(x): 0.34, 0.21, 0.13, 0.04, 0.01
Valid only if (1) every \(0\le P(x)\le 1\) AND (2) \(\sum P(x)=1\). Write BOTH checks.

Rule 1: 0.34, 0.21, 0.13, 0.04, 0.01 are all between 0 and 1 ✓   Rule 2: add them:

\(0.34+0.21+0.13+0.04+0.01=0.73\neq 1\;✗\)
NOT a valid probability distribution — the probabilities add to 0.73, not 1.
If they switch it up: a negative P(x) or one above 1 → not valid even if the sum is 1  ·  sum above 1 → not valid  ·  "find the missing probability" → 1 − (sum of the others): if P(4) were blank, P(4) = 1 − 0.72 = 0.28

Q3 (8 pts) — Stanley Cup: discrete random variable

"The following data represent the number of games played, X, in the Stanley Cup before a champion was determined." x: 4, 5, 6, 7 with frequency 20, 20, 26, 17. (a) Construct a probability distribution for X. (b) Graph it. (c) Compute and interpret the mean. (d) Compute the standard deviation.
(a) construct the probability distribution
\(P(x)=\dfrac{\text{frequency}}{\text{total}}\)   total = 20 + 20 + 26 + 17 = 83
\(P(4)=\dfrac{20}{83}=0.2410\qquad P(5)=\dfrac{20}{83}=0.2410\)
\(P(6)=\dfrac{26}{83}=0.3133\qquad P(7)=\dfrac{17}{83}=0.2048\)
Check: 0.2410 + 0.2410 + 0.3133 + 0.2048 = 1.0001 ≈ 1 ✓ (rounding).
(b) graph the discrete probability distribution
x across, P(x) up; a vertical bar (or line) above each x whose height is its probability; label both axes.
(c) compute and interpret the mean of X
\(\mu_X=\sum x\cdot P(x)\) — a weighted average: each x counts as much as its probability
\(\mu=4(0.2410)+5(0.2410)+6(0.3133)+7(0.2048)\)
\(=0.9640+1.2050+1.8798+1.4336=5.48\text{ games}\)
μ ≈ 5.48 games Interpretation (part of the points): over many Stanley Cup series, the average length is about 5.48 games.
(d) compute the standard deviation of X
\(\sigma_X=\sqrt{\sum (x-\mu)^2\cdot P(x)}\) with μ = 5.48: subtract, square, × P(x), add, √
xx − μ(x − μ)²(x − μ)² · P(x)
4−1.482.19042.1904(0.2410) = 0.5279
5−0.480.23040.2304(0.2410) = 0.0555
60.520.27040.2704(0.3133) = 0.0847
71.522.31042.3104(0.2048) = 0.4732
\(\sigma^2=0.5279+0.0555+0.0847+0.4732=1.1413\)
\(\sigma=\sqrt{1.1413}=1.07\text{ games}\)
σ ≈ 1.07 games
Watch out: don't stop at the variance 1.1413; no ÷ N here — the P(x) do the averaging.
If they switch it up: P(at least 6 games) = P(6) + P(7) = 0.3133 + 0.2048 = 0.5181  ·  P(fewer than 6) = P(4) + P(5) = 0.4820  ·  variance = σ² = 1.1413 (stop before the √)  ·  expected value E(X) = the mean

Q4 (8 pts) — Driving age: binomial

"According to a Gallup poll, 60% of U.S. women 18 years old or older stated that the minimum driving age should be 18. In a random sample of 10 U.S. women, find the probability that (a) exactly 3, (b) fewer than 3, (c) between 1 and 3 (inclusive) believe this. (d) For a random sample of 200, find the expected number and standard deviation."

Binomial: fixed n = 10 yes/no trials, independent, same p. n = 10, p = 0.60, 1 − p = 0.40, X = number who say yes.

\(P(x)={}_{n}C_{x}\;p^{x}\,(1-p)^{\,n-x}\qquad {}_{n}C_{x}=\dfrac{n!}{x!\,(n-x)!}\)
(a) exactly 3 believe the minimum driving age should be 18
\({}_{10}C_{3}=\dfrac{10!}{3!\,7!}=\dfrac{10\cdot 9\cdot 8}{3\cdot 2\cdot 1}=120\)
\(P(3)={}_{10}C_{3}\,(0.60)^{3}(0.40)^{7}=120(0.216)(0.0016384)=0.0425\)
0.0425
Watch out: the exponent on 0.40 is n − x = 7, not 3; keep all decimals until the end.
(b) fewer than 3 believe…
"fewer than 3" = 0, 1, 2 — the 3 is OUT. Add the three separate probabilities.
\(P(0)={}_{10}C_{0}(0.6)^{0}(0.4)^{10}=1(1)(0.0001049)=0.0001\)
\(P(1)={}_{10}C_{1}(0.6)^{1}(0.4)^{9}=10(0.6)(0.0002621)=0.0016\)
\(P(2)={}_{10}C_{2}(0.6)^{2}(0.4)^{8}=45(0.36)(0.0006554)=0.0106\)
\(P(X<3)=0.0001+0.0016+0.0106=0.0123\)
0.0123
(c) between 1 and 3 (inclusive) believe…
inclusive → x = 1, 2, 3. Reuse (a) and (b) — write "from part (a)".
\(P(1\le X\le 3)=P(1)+P(2)+P(3)=0.0016+0.0106+0.0425=0.0547\)
0.0547
(d) sample of 200: expected number and standard deviation
binomial shortcuts: \(\mu=np,\qquad \sigma=\sqrt{np(1-p)}\)
\(\mu=np=200(0.60)=120\)
\(\sigma=\sqrt{np(1-p)}=\sqrt{200(0.60)(0.40)}=\sqrt{48}=6.93\)
μ = 120, σ ≈ 6.93 (48 is the variance — take the √)
If they switch it up: at least 3 = 1 − P(X ≤ 2) = 1 − 0.0123 = 0.9877  ·  at most 3 = P(X ≤ 2) + P(3) = 0.0123 + 0.0425 = 0.0548  ·  more than 3 = 1 − 0.0548 = 0.9452  ·  "would 130 of 200 be unusual?" np(1 − p) = 48 ≥ 10 ✓ → usual range μ ± 2σ = 120 ± 2(6.93) = 106.1 to 133.9 → 130 is inside → not unusual

Q5 (5 pts) — Daily temperature: uniform

"The daytime temperature X (°F) in a coastal city during summer is uniformly distributed between 70°F and 100°F. (a) Sketch or describe the graph of the uniform pdf. (b) Find P(75 ≤ X ≤ 85). (c) Find P(X < 80)."
a = 70, b = 100, width b − a = 30.  \(\text{height}=\dfrac{1}{b-a}\qquad P(c\le X\le d)=\dfrac{d-c}{b-a}\) = the piece's share of the whole width. No z-scores here.
(a) sketch / describe the pdf
\(\text{height}=\dfrac{1}{b-a}=\dfrac{1}{100-70}=\dfrac{1}{30}\qquad \text{area}=30\cdot\dfrac{1}{30}=1\;✓\)
a flat line (rectangle) at height 1/30 from 70 to 100, and 0 everywhere else
(b) P(75 ≤ X ≤ 85)
\(P(75\le X\le 85)=\dfrac{85-75}{100-70}=\dfrac{10}{30}=0.3333\)
0.3333
(c) P(X < 80)
\(P(X<80)=\dfrac{80-70}{100-70}=\dfrac{10}{30}=0.3333\)
0.3333
Watch out: P(X ≤ 80) is the same number — one point has no width, P(X = 80) = 0.
If they switch it up: P(X > 90) = \(\dfrac{100-90}{30}=0.3333\)  ·  "20% of days are cooler than what temperature?" → \(x=a+P(b-a)=70+0.20(30)=76^\circ\text{F}\)  ·  P(X = 80) = 0

Q6 (6 pts) — Standard normal: the table gives the area to the LEFT

"Use the standard normal table. (a) Find the area to the left of z = −1.10. (b) Find the area between z = −0.60 and z = 1.45. (c) X is normally distributed with μ = 80 and σ = 10. Find P(70 < X < 95)."
LEFT = table value · RIGHT = 1 − table · BETWEEN = (left of the bigger z) − (left of the smaller z) · \(z=\dfrac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\) rounded to 2 decimals
(a) area to the left of z = −1.10
Row −1.1, column .00 → read the entry: 0.1357
Watch out: positive-only table? symmetry: 1 − (left of +1.10) = 1 − 0.8643 = 0.1357.
(b) area between z = −0.60 and z = 1.45
left of 1.45 (row 1.4, col .05) = 0.9265; left of −0.60 = 0.2743.
\(\text{between}=0.9265-0.2743=0.6522\)
0.6522
Watch out: subtract the AREAS, never the z's; a negative answer means you subtracted the wrong way.
(c) μ = 80, σ = 10: P(70 < X < 95)
\(z_1=\dfrac{70-80}{10}=-1.00\ \to\ \text{left}=0.1587\)
\(z_2=\dfrac{95-80}{10}=1.50\ \to\ \text{left}=0.9332\)
\(P(70<X<95)=0.9332-0.1587=0.7745\)
0.7745
If they switch it up: right of z = 1.45 = 1 − 0.9265 = 0.0735  ·  P(X > 95) = 1 − 0.9332 = 0.0668  ·  z with area 0.10 to its LEFT → closest entry 0.1003 → z = −1.28  ·  z with area 0.025 to its RIGHT → left area 0.975 → z = 1.96

Q7 (8 pts) — Wechsler IQ: normal with μ = 100, σ = 15

"The Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children is approximately normally distributed with mean μ = 100 and standard deviation σ = 15. (a) P(score above 125)? (b) P(score below 90)? (c) What proportion score between 110 and 140? (d) What score places a child in the 98th percentile?"
Forward (a)–(c): \(z=\dfrac{x-100}{15}\) → table → below: left · above: 1 − left · between: bigger left − smaller left.   Backward (d): area → z (found INSIDE the table) → \(x=\mu+z\sigma\)
(a) probability a test taker scores above 125
\(z=\dfrac{125-100}{15}=1.67\ \to\ \text{left}=0.9525\)
\(P(X>125)=1-0.9525=0.0475\)
0.0475
Watch out: 0.9525 is the #1 wrong answer; 125 is above average → answer below 0.5.
(b) probability a test taker scores below 90
\(z=\dfrac{90-100}{15}=-0.67\)
\(P(X<90)=\text{left of }-0.67=0.2514\)
0.2514 (keep the minus sign on z)
(c) proportion scoring between 110 and 140
\(z_1=\dfrac{110-100}{15}=0.67\ \to\ \text{left}=0.7486\)
\(z_2=\dfrac{140-100}{15}=2.67\ \to\ \text{left}=0.9962\)
\(P(110<X<140)=0.9962-0.7486=0.2476\)
0.2476 (proportion = probability; 4 decimals)
(d) score at the 98th percentile
98th percentile = 98% score below it → area to the LEFT = 0.98. Find 0.98 INSIDE the table: 0.9798 (z = 2.05) is closer than 0.9803 (z = 2.06) → z = 2.05.
\(x=\mu+z\sigma=100+2.05(15)\)
\(=100+30.75=130.75\approx 131\)
≈ 131 (130.75)
Watch out: never put 0.98 into the z formula — it is an area, not a score.
If they switch it up: top 5% → left 0.95 → z = 1.645 → x = 100 + 1.645(15) = 124.7  ·  bottom 10% → left 0.10 → z = −1.28 → x = 100 − 1.28(15) = 80.8  ·  middle 95% → z = ±1.96 → 70.6 to 129.4  ·  is a score of 70 unusual? z = −2.00 → 0.0228 < 0.05 → yes

EXAM 1 SKILLS THAT COME BACK

\(\mu=\dfrac{\sum x}{N}\qquad \bar x=\dfrac{\sum x}{n}\qquad \sigma=\sqrt{\dfrac{\sum (x-\mu)^2}{N}}\qquad s=\sqrt{\dfrac{\sum (x-\bar x)^2}{n-1}}\)
"sample" → divide by n − 1 · calculator: σx = population SD, Sx = sample SD
  • Mean = the balance point; median = middle of the sorted list (resistant to outliers); mode = most frequent value. SD = typical distance from the mean: subtract the mean, square (the gaps add to 0 otherwise), average the squares (= variance), √.
  • z-score \(z=\dfrac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\) = how many SDs above (+) or below (−) the mean; compare different scales: the bigger z did relatively better (if lower is better, the smaller z wins); z beyond ±2 → unusual. ACT 27 (μ 18, σ 6): z = 1.50 beats SAT 1200 (μ 1000, σ 200): z = 1.00.
  • Empirical Rule 68–95–99.7 (picture in the normal section). Quartiles Q₂ = median, Q₁/Q₃ = medians of the halves; IQR = Q₃ − Q₁; fences Q₁ − 1.5·IQR and Q₃ + 1.5·IQR; outside → outlier

CH 4 — PROBABILITY

  • Probability = the long-run fraction of times something happens (Law of Large Numbers: more trials → the fraction settles at the probability). 0 ≤ P(E) ≤ 1; all outcomes together add to 1. Unusual event = probability below 0.05.
  • Sample space S = everything that could happen; an event = any part of it. Two coins: S = {HH, HT, TH, TT}.
  • Classical (equally likely outcomes — dice, cards, coins): count. \(P(E)=\dfrac{N(E)}{N(S)}\) P(two heads) = 1/4; P(at least one tail) = 3/4.
  • Empirical (from data): \(P(E)\approx\dfrac{\text{frequency of }E}{\text{number of trials}}\) 22 of 200 carpool → 0.11; 5 walk → 0.025 < 0.05 → unusual. Subjective = educated guess.
  • Disjoint (mutually exclusive) = can't both happen (king AND queen), so P(E and F) = 0. Independent = E happening doesn't change P(F) (two coin flips). Disjoint is NOT independent: if they are disjoint, knowing E happened means F didn't.

The rules — spot the keyword

NOT: \(P(E^{c})=1-P(E)\)  ·  AT LEAST ONE: \(P(\text{at least 1})=1-P(\text{none})\)
OR: \(P(E\text{ or }F)=P(E)+P(F)-P(E\text{ and }F)\)  (disjoint → the overlap is 0, just add)
AND, independent: \(P(E\text{ and }F)=P(E)\cdot P(F)\)  ·  AND, general: \(P(E\text{ and }F)=P(E)\cdot P(F\mid E)\)
GIVEN: \(P(F\mid E)=\dfrac{P(E\text{ and }F)}{P(E)}=\dfrac{N(E\text{ and }F)}{N(E)}\)  ·  Independent? check \(P(F\mid E)=P(F)\): equal → independent, different → dependent
  • "At least one": compute the one case you don't want (none) and subtract from 1. P(survive) = 0.9986: P(both of 2 survive) = 0.9986² = 0.9972; P(at least one of 1000 dies) = 1 − 0.99861000 = 0.7536. Without replacement from a big group: sample under 5% of the population → treat as independent.

Counting — "how many ways"

  • A sequence of choices: multiply the options at each step. 3-letter codes: 26·26·26 = 17,576; chair, vice-chair, secretary from 14: 14·13·12 = 2,184.
  • Permutation = pick r of n, ORDER MATTERS (1st/2nd/3rd). \({}_{n}P_{r}=\dfrac{n!}{(n-r)!}\) ₁₀P₃ = 10·9·8 = 720; 7 in order: 7! = 5,040.
  • Combination = pick r of n, order DOESN'T matter (committee, hand, sample). \({}_{n}C_{r}=\dfrac{n!}{r!\,(n-r)!}\) ₄C₂ = 24/(2·2) = 6; ₂₀C₄ = 4,845. Probability by counting: P(4 cards all the same rank) = 13 ÷ ₅₂C₄ = 13/270,725 ≈ 0.00005.

CH 5 — DISCRETE RANDOM VARIABLES ★Q2 ★Q3

  • Random variable X = the number that comes out of a chance experiment. Discrete = you count it (0, 1, 2, …); continuous = you measure it (time, temperature). Probability distribution = every value X can take with its probability.
VALID only if (1) every \(0\le P(x)\le 1\) AND (2) \(\sum P(x)=1\)
\(\mu_X=E(X)=\sum\big[x\cdot P(x)\big]\)
\(\sigma_X=\sqrt{\sum\big[(x-\mu)^2\cdot P(x)\big]}\)

Mean of a distribution = weighted average (each value counts as much as its probability) = the long-run average per trial. SD = typical distance from the mean, each squared gap weighted by its probability.

  1. From counts: P(x) = count ÷ total; check they add to 1.
  2. Mean: multiply each x by its P(x); add.
  3. SD: (x − μ)² for each x; × P(x); add; √.
  4. Graph: a bar above each x, height P(x).
Q: "Insurance: +$100 with 0.9986, −$9,900 with 0.0014. Expected profit?" A: 100(0.9986) − 9900(0.0014) = 99.86 − 13.86 = $86 per policy (positive → keep selling).

CH 5 (cont.) — BINOMIAL ★Q4

Binomial experiment = the same yes/no trial repeated: (1) FIXED number of trials n, (2) independent, (3) two outcomes (success / failure), (4) SAME p each time. X = number of successes. Not binomial if n isn't fixed ("until…") or picks are without replacement from a small group.

\(P(x)={}_{n}C_{x}\;p^{x}(1-p)^{\,n-x}\qquad {}_{n}C_{x}=\dfrac{n!}{x!\,(n-x)!}\)
\(\mu=np\qquad \sigma=\sqrt{np(1-p)}\)
Unusual count (when np(1 − p) ≥ 10): below μ − 2σ or above μ + 2σ  ·  Excel: =BINOM.DIST(x, n, p, FALSE); TRUE = P(X ≤ x)
Q: "Binomial or not? (1) Roll a pair of dice 10 times, count sums of 7. (2) Select flights until 10 late ones. (3) Pick 4 of 30 students, count females." A: (1) YES, n = 10, p = 6/36. (2) NO, n not fixed. (3) NO, without replacement from 30 (13% > 5%).

CH 6 — CONTINUOUS & NORMAL

  • A continuous variable has infinitely many values, so probability = AREA under the curve (the pdf: never below 0, total area 1). P(X = one exact value) = 0 so P(a < X < b) = P(a ≤ X ≤ b).

Uniform ★Q5

Uniform on [a, b] = a flat rectangle: every stretch of the same length is equally likely. Probability of an interval = its share of the total width.

\(\text{height}=\dfrac{1}{b-a}\qquad P(c\le X\le d)=\dfrac{d-c}{b-a}\)
\(\text{value with area }P\text{ to its left: } x=a+P\,(b-a)\)

Normal ★Q6 ★Q7

Normal = the bell curve: symmetric around μ, most values near the middle. μ slides it left/right, σ makes it wider or narrower. Properties: symmetric about μ; mean = median = mode = μ (the peak); inflection points at μ ± σ; total area 1; tails never touch the axis; Empirical Rule; half the area on each side of μ.

Standard normal Z = μ = 0, σ = 1. The trick: convert any value to a z-score and use ONE table (Table V), which gives the area to the LEFT of z.

\(z=\dfrac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\ \ (\text{round to 2 decimals})\qquad x=\mu+z\sigma\)
LEFT ("less than / below"): table value  ·  RIGHT ("more than / above"): 1 − table value
BETWEEN x₁ and x₂: (left of z₂) − (left of z₁)  ·  negative z: left of −z = 1 − (left of +z)

Steps: draw the bell, mark μ and x, shade → z = (x − μ)/σ to 2 decimals → table: row = first two digits (1.6), column = last digit (.07) = area to the LEFT → left: done · right: 1 − area · between: subtract the two lefts.

Backwards — find x from a percent / percentile ★Q7(d)

The area is GIVEN; run the steps backwards: words → area to the LEFT ("top 10%" → 0.90, "bottom 5%" → 0.05, "k-th percentile" → k/100, "middle 80%" → 0.10 and 0.90) → find it INSIDE the table (closest entry; exactly halfway → average the two z's: 0.95 → 1.645, 0.995 → 2.575) → read z from the edges → x = μ + zσ.

Percentile → z: 1st −2.33 · 2nd −2.05 · 2.5th −1.96 · 5th −1.645 · 10th −1.28 · 20th −0.84 · 25th −0.67 · 50th 0 · 75th 0.67 · 80th 0.84 · 90th 1.28 · 95th 1.645 · 97.5th 1.96 · 98th 2.05 · 99th 2.33
Middle 80% ↔ z = ±1.28 · middle 90% ↔ ±1.645 · middle 95% ↔ ±1.96 · middle 99% ↔ ±2.575
  • "Give two interpretations" of an area: "the area to the right of 200 is 0.2903" means (1) the proportion of the population above 200 is 0.2903 (29.03%) AND (2) the probability that one randomly selected person is above 200 is 0.2903.
  • Is it normal? histogram bell-shaped, one peak, symmetric, or a normal probability plot close to a straight line → approximately normal; a long tail → skewed, not normal.

DON'T LOSE POINTS

  • "Or" → subtract the overlap once. "And" → the one cell. "Given" → divide by the given group, not the grand total. Σ P(x) = 1 and each P(x) 0–1 before calling a distribution valid.
  • "Fewer than 3" stops at 2; "at least 3" = 1 − P(X ≤ 2); "at most" includes the number. Binomial σ needs the √. Uniform: length ÷ total width, never z.
  • Normal: z to 2 decimals; table = area to the LEFT; "above" → 1 − left; "between" → big − small. Percentile → area → z (inside the table) → x = μ + zσ. 4 decimals; formula every time.
