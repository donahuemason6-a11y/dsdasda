# Video Discussion Script: The Stanford Prison Experiment

**Target length:** about 8 to 9 minutes spoken at a normal conversational pace (roughly 1,500 words).
The requirement is 5 to 10 minutes, so this leaves room on both sides. If you talk fast,
slow down on the quotes and pause between sections. If you run long, trim Section 7 or shorten the Milgram comparison in Section 3.

**Delivery tips:**
- Don't read word for word. Glance at each paragraph, look up, and say it in your own voice.
- Pause about one second wherever you see `[pause]`.
- Time markers are approximate. Check your recording after the first take.

---

## Section 1: Opening (about 0:00 to 0:30)

Hi everyone. For this discussion I'm going to talk about the Stanford Prison Experiment
documentary. I'll be honest, I thought I already knew this study, but hearing the actual
participants describe it years later was a very different experience from reading a
summary. I'm going to go through the questions roughly in order, and a couple of my answers
changed while I was thinking them through.

[pause]

## Section 2: What surprised me most (about 0:30 to 1:55)

What surprised me most was not the guards. I expected the guards to become cruel, because
that's the part of the study everyone knows. What surprised me was how quickly the prisoners
stopped acting like volunteers.

Take prisoner 8612. He leads the rebellion on the second morning, then asks to leave. And
instead of simply letting him go, Zimbardo offers him a deal. He says he'll keep the guards
from hassling him if 8612 gives him information about the other prisoners. He asks him to
be an informer. 8612 goes back to his cell confused and tells the others that nobody is
allowed to leave. That was never true, but everyone believed it. To me, that's the moment
it stopped being a study for them. A few hours later he's screaming that he's burning up
inside, and they have to release him.

Then there's 819, which affected me even more. Zimbardo tells him directly that he can go.
He hears the other prisoners chanting that 819 is a bad prisoner, and he starts crying and
says he can't leave. He wants to go back to prove he's not a bad prisoner. Zimbardo has to
tell him, you're not a prisoner, this is not a prison. That was after about three days. In
three days a college student forgot he could walk out the door.

And finally the blanket vote. The guards offer the prisoners a choice: give up your blankets
and 416 gets out of the hole, or keep them and he stays another night. Most of them kept the
blanket. There was no solidarity at all, and that was hard to watch.

## Section 3: Was it ethical, and the Milgram comparison (about 1:55 to 3:55)

Was it ethical? No, and I don't think it's a close call. The participants never agreed to
what the guards ended up doing, because nobody knew in advance what the guards would do.
The prisoners came to believe they couldn't quit, which is the one thing a study is supposed
to guarantee. And there was no one independent with the authority to stop it. Look at how it
actually ended. A colleague walked in, saw the prisoners chained together with bags over
their heads being marched to the toilet, and confronted him. She told him these were young
men suffering and he was responsible. His reaction was, oh my god, you're right. So the
study didn't end because of the data. It ended because someone with fresh eyes was
horrified.

[pause]

Now, the comparison to Milgram. The documentary spends a lot of time on the shock study,
and I understand why. Two thirds of participants went all the way to the highest voltage
because a man in a lab coat kept telling them the experiment required them to continue.
Both studies deceived people, and both caused real distress.

But for me, Stanford was more problematic, for one main reason. Milgram's harm had a
ceiling. You were in the room for an hour, the person being shocked was an actor, and
afterward you were shown he was fine. The damage was mostly learning something about
yourself you didn't want to know. Stanford had no ceiling. It ran around the clock for six
days, the suffering was real, and the person who should have stopped it was the same person
running the prison. Milgram was a deceptive experiment. Stanford, by about day three, had
stopped being an experiment at all. Zimbardo admits this himself. He says he had completely
lost his identity as a scientist and a psychologist.

## Section 4: How I would have reacted as a prisoner (about 3:55 to 4:55)

How would I have reacted as a prisoner? I'd like to say I would have been 416, the
replacement who refused to eat and went on a hunger strike and never broke. That's who I'd
want to be.

But honestly, I'm not sure. 416 came in fresh. He hadn't been worn down. The prisoners
who'd been there from the start were being woken every few hours for counts, there was no
daylight so they couldn't tell night from day, and they had already watched two people break
down and get taken away. One of the guards says directly that the goal was to make sure
prisoners never knew what would happen next. Under those conditions, I think I would have
done what most of them did. Keep my head down and keep my blanket. I don't like that
answer, but I think it's the honest one, and I think that's really the point of the study.

## Section 5: How I would have reacted as a guard (about 4:55 to 5:55)

The guard question is harder, because the way I see it there were three kinds of guards.

There was the one nicknamed John Wayne, Dave Eshelman, who says he decided to be as cold
and cruel as possible and modeled himself on the prison warden from a movie he'd just seen.
There were guards who did the job but didn't invent new ways to humiliate people. And there
were the guards who hated what they saw, who never did anything degrading themselves, but
also never said a word to stop it. One of them essentially became the errand runner, going
out for the food so he wouldn't have to watch.

I don't think I would have been Eshelman. But I think I probably would have been that third
kind of guard. What stayed with me was Eshelman saying that the thing that shocked him most
was that no one ever questioned his authority. Not one person. Everyone seemed to assume
someone else would step in, or that the psychologists watching must be fine with it. So I'd
like to think I would have spoken up. But the film is essentially a warning that most people
in that position don't.

[pause]

## Section 6: What Zimbardo should have done, and how much guilt (about 5:55 to 7:15)

What should he have done differently? Three things.

First, he should not have been the prison superintendent. He says this himself. He should
have been the researcher with someone else running the prison, or run the prison with a
colleague above him who could shut it down at any moment. Remember the scene where another
psychologist asks him what his independent variable is, and he gets furious because in his
mind a prison break is about to happen. That's not a researcher anymore. That's a warden.

Second, he shouldn't have briefed the guards the way he did. He told them to create fear,
to make the prisoners feel their lives were totally controlled, that the guards had all the
power and the prisoners had none. Then he presents what the guards did as if it emerged on
its own. Eshelman even says he figured out the study was meant to show prisons are cruel,
so he decided to help those results come about. He calls it his own little experiment. That
seriously undercuts the conclusion, because at least one of the worst guards was
consciously performing.

Third, the right to leave should have been non-negotiable. The moment 8612 said he wanted
out, he should have been out. No deal, no informer offer.

How much guilt should he have? A fair amount, I think. Not for the idea. The question was
legitimate, and the rules in 1971 were different. But for the decisions he made during those
six days, yes. He was the one adult in the building whose job was to protect those students,
and he needed someone else to walk in and tell him they were suffering. I'll give him credit
for spending decades openly discussing what he got wrong. That counts for something. It
doesn't erase it.

## Section 7: If it were conducted today (about 7:15 to 8:00)

Last question. If this were run today, the first thing to say is that it couldn't be. No
review board would approve it now. But suppose it could.

I think the results would be less extreme, for a few reasons. Everyone would know they were
being recorded and that the footage could end up public, which changes behavior. Most
students have at least heard of this study, so they'd know how it's supposed to go. And I
think the guards would hesitate more, because attitudes toward that kind of abuse have
shifted a lot since 1971.

But I don't think the core finding would disappear. Give people uniforms, anonymity, sleep
deprivation, and someone in authority telling them it's acceptable, and I think you would
still see some people go further than they ever expected, and a lot of people stay silent.
The setting would look different. The human behavior probably wouldn't.

## Section 8: Closing (about 8:00 to 8:20)

So where I land is this. The situation is powerful, and I think most of us would behave
worse in that basement than we'd like to admit. But I also think the study tells us less
about human nature than Zimbardo claimed, and more about what happens when the person
running an experiment becomes part of it.

The question I'd put to the rest of you is the one I couldn't answer cleanly myself. If you
had been one of the quiet guards, do you honestly think you would have said something?

Thanks for listening.
