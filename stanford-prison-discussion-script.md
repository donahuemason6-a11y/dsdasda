# Discussion Script: The Stanford Prison Experiment

**Target length:** about 5 to 5.5 minutes at your pace (roughly 700 words).
The requirement is 5 to 10 minutes. If your first take comes in under 5 minutes, slow down
slightly and hold the pauses. Do not cut anything.

**Delivery tips:**
- Don't read word for word. Glance at each paragraph, look up, and say it in your own voice.
- Pause about one second wherever you see `[pause]`.
- Time markers are approximate. Check your recording after the first take.

## Section 1: Opening (about 0:00 to 0:15)

Hi everyone. Today I am going to talk about the Stanford Prison Experiment and go through
the questions one at a time.

## Section 2: What surprised me most (about 0:15 to 1:05)

What surprised me most was not the guards. I knew they would be cruel. What surprised me
was how fast the prisoners believed they were real prisoners.

Prisoner 8612 asked to leave on the second day. Instead of letting him go, Zimbardo offered
him a deal to be an informer. 8612 went back and told everyone that nobody could leave.
That was not true, but they all believed it. A few hours later he broke down.

Prisoner 819 was even worse. Zimbardo told him he could go. But the other prisoners were
chanting that he was a bad prisoner, so he cried and said he could not leave. That was only
three days in.

## Section 3: Was it ethical, and how it compares to Milgram (about 1:05 to 2:15)

No, I do not think it was ethical. The participants did not know how far the guards would
go. The prisoners believed they could not quit. And nobody outside the study had the power
to stop it. It only ended when a colleague saw the prisoners chained together with bags on
their heads and told Zimbardo he was responsible.

Compared to Milgram, I think Stanford was worse. In Milgram's study the harm had a limit. It
lasted an hour, the person being shocked was an actor, and afterward people saw he was fine.
Stanford went on day and night for six days. The suffering was real. And the person who
should have stopped it was the same person running the prison.

[pause]

## Section 4: As a prisoner (about 2:15 to 2:55)

If I was a prisoner, I would like to say I would have been like 416, who went on a hunger
strike and never gave up. But he came in fresh. The others were woken up every few hours,
could not see daylight, and had already watched two people break down. Under those
conditions, I think I would have done what most of them did. Keep quiet and keep my
blanket. I do not like that answer, but I think it is the honest one.

## Section 5: As a guard (about 2:55 to 3:40)

As a guard, I do not think I would have been like Eshelman, the one they called John Wayne,
who chose on purpose to be as cruel as possible. But I think I would have been one of the
guards who did not like it and still said nothing. Eshelman said the most shocking part for
him was that nobody ever told him to stop. Not one person. I would like to think I would
have spoken up. But this study shows that most people in that spot do not.

## Section 6: What Zimbardo should have done, and how much guilt (about 3:40 to 4:35)

Zimbardo should have done three things differently. First, he should not have played the
prison superintendent. Someone else should have had the power to stop the study. Second, he
should not have told the guards to create fear and take away the prisoners' control, and
then act like they came up with it on their own. Third, anyone who asked to leave should
have been let out right away. No deals.

How much guilt should he have? A lot. Not for the idea, but for the choices he made during
those six days. It was his job to protect those students, and he needed someone else to
walk in and tell him they were suffering.

## Section 7: If it were done today (about 4:35 to 5:05)

If this were done today, it would not be allowed. But if it somehow happened, I think it
would be less extreme. People would know they were being recorded, and most students
already know how this study turned out. Still, if you give people uniforms, take away their
names, and have someone in charge say it is okay, I think some people would still go too
far, and most would still stay quiet.

## Section 8: Closing (about 5:05 to 5:20)

So overall, the situation was powerful, but I think the study says more about what happens
when the person in charge becomes part of it. My question for you is, if you were one of
the guards who did not like what was happening, do you honestly think you would have said
something? Thank you.

# Peer Replies

Each reply is about 210 words, which runs roughly 80 to 90 seconds spoken. The minimum is
one minute, so keep a normal pace and do not rush. Replace [Name] with the classmate's name.

## Reply 1: To the classmate who said they would have treated prisoners with respect as a guard

Hi [Name]. I agreed with a lot of what you said, especially your point about how quickly
the power dynamic was established. That surprised me as well. The example that stood out to
me was prisoner 819, who was told he could leave and refused because he did not want the
other prisoners to think he was a bad prisoner. That happened after about three days, which
shows how fast the roles took hold.

The one place I would push back a little is on the guard question. You said you would have
treated the prisoners with respect, and I believe you. But there were guards in the study
who felt exactly that way. They never degraded anyone themselves. The problem is that none
of them ever spoke up or intervened while the harsher guards escalated. So I think the
harder question is not whether we would have been respectful, but whether we would have
actually said something to the other guards.

I also agree with you that there should have been clearer rules and someone with the
authority to stop the study. I would add one specific rule: the right to leave should have
been absolute. When 8612 asked to go, Zimbardo offered him a deal instead of releasing him,
and I think that was the turning point. Thanks for your response.

## Reply 2: To the classmate who discussed groupthink and said the results would be the same today

Hi [Name]. I thought your point about groupthink and group cohesion was a strong one, and I
agree with your conclusion that Milgram's study was more ethical than Stanford. My reasoning
was similar to yours. Milgram had far more control over the setting, each session was
short, and the person being shocked was never actually harmed, while the Stanford study ran
for six days with real suffering and no one independent to stop it.

One thing I would add to your groupthink point is that at least one guard, Dave Eshelman,
said he deliberately decided to be as cruel as possible and even described it as his own
experiment. So the abuse was not entirely the group drifting together. One person pushed it,
and the rest of the group went along with him. To me that supports your point about
cohesion even more, because nobody questioned him.

Where I see it a bit differently is your view that the results would be pretty much the
same today. I think the core behavior would still appear, but I believe it would be less
extreme, partly for the reasons you mentioned: full disclosure, more oversight, and
participants knowing what the study is designed to show. So I think we agree that it would
never reach the point the original did. Thanks for your response.
