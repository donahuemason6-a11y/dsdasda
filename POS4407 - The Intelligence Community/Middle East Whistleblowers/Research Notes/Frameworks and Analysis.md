## Key Question 1: How do scholars of intelligence ethics define national-security whistleblowing, and how do they distinguish a whistleblower from a leaker, spy, defector or dissident?

### Takeaway
The literature converges on a core definition (an insider who discloses evidence of illegality or serious abuse, through a channel capable of acting on it, for public rather than private ends) but splits sharply on three contested criteria: whether internal channels must be exhausted first, whether the discloser must identify themselves, and whether policy disagreement ever qualifies. The Tshwane Principles (2013) and the European Court of Human Rights' Guja criteria (2008) supply the most-cited "public-interest tests" the student can apply to Middle Eastern cases; the "defector" and "spy" labels turn on a separate axis (allegiance and recipient) from the "whistleblower" label (content, channel and motive).

### Cited Findings

**Baseline social-science definition**
- The foundational organizational definition is Near and Miceli (1984): whistleblowing is "the disclosure by an organization's members, current or former, of illegal, unethical, or illegitimate practices to parties who can act on them." Legal protection "typically attaches to the channel used and the good-faith belief that a law or rule was actually broken, not just to the act of disclosure itself"; someone who "simply dislikes a policy, disagrees with a classification decision, or objects to a program on ideological grounds has not, by that fact alone, blown a whistle in any legal or scholarly sense." — [ClearanceJobs, "Snowden, Manning and Teixeira: when does leaking become whistleblowing" (Aug 2026)](https://news.clearancejobs.com/2026/08/18/snowden-manning-and-teixeira-when-does-leaking-become-whistleblowing/)

**Rahul Sagar, Secrets and Leaks: The Dilemma of State Secrecy (Princeton UP, 2013)**
- Sagar argues "that though whistleblowing can be morally justified, the fear of retaliation usually prompts officials to act anonymously—that is, to 'leak' information," making it "difficult for the public to discern when an unauthorized disclosure is intended to further partisan interests." Because such disclosures "are the only credible means of checking the executive... they must be tolerated, and, at times, even celebrated," but the public should "treat such disclosures skeptically." — [Princeton UP summary via HCU catalog](https://katalog.hcu-hamburg.de/vufind/Record/1657833496); [Lawfare review by Steven Aftergood](https://www.lawfaremedia.org/resources/lawfare-news/secrets-and-leaks-dilemma-state-secrecy-rahul-sagar)
- Sagar's five conditions for a justified unauthorized disclosure, as summarized by reviewers: (1) it "must disclose genuine wrongdoing, which is more often contestable than clear. In particular, a disagreement over policy is not the same as an accusation of illegality, though all too often self-appointed whistleblowers collapse the distinction"; (2) it "must be based on evidence rather than hearsay" (clear and convincing evidence); (3) it must not pose a disproportionate threat to public safety; (4) it "must be limited in scale and scope as much as possible"; (5) the discloser should be prepared to be identified, although "Anonymous disclosures are justified when they reveal gross wrongdoing." — [Hudson Institute review](https://www.hudson.org/national-security-defense/whistleblowers-and-traitors-a-review-of-secrets-and-leaks-by-rahul-sagar); [Lawfare](https://www.lawfaremedia.org/resources/lawfare-news/secrets-and-leaks-dilemma-state-secrecy-rahul-sagar); [Jack Shafer review](https://www.jackshafer.com/book_reviews/20140711_rahul_sagar_secrets_and_leaks.php); [John Naughton summary](https://memex.naughtons.org/?p=19677)
- The New Statesman reading: "The ideal whistleblower would be cautious, first contacting their boss and only later going to the press"; anonymity is acceptable only for "gross wrongdoing" (Sagar's example is Abu Ghraib); in ordinary cases of "suspected" wrongdoing "an assessment of the whistleblowers' intentions is required" and they "must not be partisan, driven by morality, politics or revenge." — [New Statesman, "Whistle while you work" (Nov 2013)](https://www.newstatesman.com/culture/2013/11/whistle-while-you-work)
- The Hudson reviewer identifies Sagar's "Catch-22": "identifying abuses of secrecy requires access to the very material being kept secret." — [Hudson Institute](https://www.hudson.org/national-security-defense/whistleblowers-and-traitors-a-review-of-secrets-and-leaks-by-rahul-sagar)
- Reviewers disagree on condition 5: Naughton/Foreign Affairs read Sagar as requiring that "the leaker must unmask himself and take his lumps to prove that he made the disclosure in good faith," while Lawfare reads an exception for gross wrongdoing. — [Naughton](https://memex.naughtons.org/?p=19677); [Foreign Affairs](https://www.foreignaffairs.com/node/1070083); [Lawfare](https://www.lawfaremedia.org/resources/lawfare-news/secrets-and-leaks-dilemma-state-secrecy-rahul-sagar)

**David Pozen, "The Leaky Leviathan" (Harvard Law Review, 2013)**
- Pozen's thesis is the gap between how US law "condemns" leaking in the abstract and how the executive "condones" it in practice: leaks function as "a safety valve, a covert messaging system, a perception management tool, and more," and criminal enforcement has been rare, with "fewer than a dozen criminal cases brought against suspected leakers in US history" at the time of writing. — [FAS summary](https://fas.org/publication/leaks_pozen/); [Columbia Law scholarship page](https://scholarship.law.columbia.edu/faculty_scholarship/770); [Lawfare "Readings"](https://www.lawfaremedia.org/article/readings-david-pozen-leaky-leviathan-why-government-condemns-and-condones-unlawful-disclosures)
- Sagar's rejoinder ("Creaky Leviathan," HLR Forum 127) argues that many unauthorized disclosures "do hurt executive policymaking," so permissive enforcement is not clearly a deliberate executive choice. — [Harvard Law Review Forum](https://harvardlawreview.org/wp-content/uploads/2013/12/forvol127_sagar.pdf)

**Kaeten Mistry and Hannah Gurman (eds.), Whistleblowing Nation (Columbia UP, 2020)**
- The volume frames a "paradox of national security whistleblowing": "whistleblowing is widely acknowledged to be an essential feature of democracy, but the US government denies its existence" in the security domain; officials framed Manning's and Snowden's acts as "unauthorized disclosures," withholding the whistleblower label. Contributors "challenge reductive characterizations of whistleblowers as heroes or traitors." Pozen contributes a coda on Snowden and civil disobedience. — [Columbia UP blog Q&A with Mistry](https://cupblog.org/2020/04/05/qa-kaeten-mistry-on-whistleblowing-nation/); [UEA book page](https://www.uea.ac.uk/groups-and-centres/american-studies/whistleblowing-nation-the-history-of-national-security-disclosures-and-the-cult-of-state-secrecy-edited-by-kaeten-mistry-and-hannah-gurman); [New Books Network](https://newbooksnetwork.com/whistleblowing-nation)

**Allison Stanger, Whistleblowers: Honesty in America from Washington to Trump (Yale UP, 2019)**
- Stanger: "just thinking oneself a whistleblower isn't enough. A whistleblower cannot be defined as 'an advocate for the change I'd like to see.' That definition confuses whistleblowing with political activism, and it invites partisan interpretations." She anchors her definition in the US Whistleblower Protection Act 1989 standard: "disclosing information that you reasonably believe is evidence of a violation of any law, rule, or regulation," gross mismanagement or a substantial danger to public safety; "For our purposes, a whistleblower is an insider." — [Yale UP excerpt](https://yalebooks.yale.edu/?p=11040)
- In interview: "the whistleblower is somebody who reveals misconduct, either illegal or unconstitutional or a threat to the public interests, health, safety"; insiders "often try to bring that to the attention of the superiors... the message often isn't heard and... then they go outside." — [NHPR, Oct 2019](https://www.nhpr.org/the-exchange/2019-10-16/whistleblowers-honesty-in-america-from-washington-to-trump)

**Tom Mueller, Crisis of Conscience: Whistleblowing in an Age of Fraud (Riverhead, 2019)**
- Based on interviews with "more than two hundred people who have exposed wrongdoing in areas such as national security, finance, and health care"; Mueller "distinguishes between 'anonymous leakers' in the Trump administration and 'authentic whistleblowers'" who stand behind claims with professional authority and personal conviction. Kirkus notes he "harshly criticizes 'national security mandarins' who abuse public trust." — [Penguin Random House](https://penguinrandomhouse.com/books/318606/crisis-of-conscience-by-tom-mueller); [Publishers Weekly](https://www.publishersweekly.com/978-1-59463-443-7); [Kirkus](https://kirkusreviews.com/book-reviews/tom-mueller/crisis-of-conscience)
- Mueller argues the Obama administration "gave Trump many of the tools he has available for continuing a war on whistleblowers," including "prosecuting truth-tellers with the Espionage Act." — [Shadowproof interview, Dec 2019](https://shadowproof.com/2019/12/09/interview-writer-tom-mueller-on-his-book-crisis-of-conscience-whistleblowing-in-an-age-of-fraud/)

**The Tshwane Principles (Global Principles on National Security and the Right to Information, 12 June 2013)**
- Drafted by 22 civil-society organizations and academic centres, facilitated by the Open Society Justice Initiative, to guide "those engaged in drafting, revising, or implementing relevant laws and policies." — [OSJI, "Understanding the Tshwane Principles"](https://www.justiceinitiative.org/publications/understanding-tshwane-principles); [ICJ launch note](https://www.icj.org/?p=26521)
- Principle 3: information may be withheld only where disclosure poses "a real and identifiable risk of significant harm to a legitimate national security interest." Under no circumstances may information about gross human-rights violations or serious violations of international humanitarian law be withheld (Principle 10). No entity, including intelligence services, may be exempt from disclosure duties; the public has a right to know about surveillance systems and their authorization procedures. — [OSJI 15-point summary PDF](https://www.justiceinitiative.org/uploads/45d4db46-e2c4-4419-932b-6b9aadad7c38/tshwane-principles-15-points-09182013.pdf); [Amnesty International statement, 2013](https://www.amnesty.org/ar/wp-content/uploads/2021/06/ior500012013en.pdf)
- Whistleblower provisions (Part VI, Principles 37-43, paraphrased from OSJI summaries): public personnel should be protected from retaliation where the public interest in disclosure outweighs the public interest in secrecy, provided they first made a reasonable effort to use internal/official mechanisms "provided that an effective mechanism exists"; criminal prosecution for disclosure should be reserved for cases posing "a real and identifiable risk of causing significant harm" that overrides the public interest. — [OSJI, "Whistleblowers and Secrets: Twelve Principles"](https://www.justiceinitiative.org/voices/whistleblowers-and-secrets-twelve-principles); [WIN resource page](https://whistleblowingnetwork.org/Learn/Resources/Resource-Items/The-Tschwane-Principles)
- Reception: UN Special Rapporteur Frank La Rue called them a major contribution to the right of access to information and the right to truth; the Council of Europe Parliamentary Assembly endorsed them (Resolution 1954 (2013)); available in Arabic. — [Free Press Unlimited knowledge base](https://kq.freepressunlimited.org/evidence/the-global-principles-on-national-security-and-the-right-to-information-tshwane-principles/); [IDFI](https://idfi.ge/en/a-question-of-public-interest)

**The European "public-interest test": Guja v. Moldova (ECtHR GC, 2008) and Halet v. Luxembourg (ECtHR GC, 2023)**
- Guja was "the first time the Court directly addressed the concept of 'whistle-blowing'"; the Grand Chamber set six criteria balancing public interest against the employee's duty of loyalty: availability of alternative (internal) channels; public interest in the information; its authenticity; good faith of the discloser; damage to the employer; severity of the sanction. — [ECtHR Factsheet: Whistleblowers](https://www.echr.coe.int/documents/d/echr/FS_Whistleblowers_ENG); [Saar Expert Paper (Yurttagül, 2024)](https://jean-monnet-saar.eu/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/Saar-Expert-Paper_Yurttaguel.pdf)
- Halet (14 Feb 2023) restated the Guja criteria (§114) and refined them "in light of the current European and international context," extending protection to private-sector employees and highlighting Luxembourg's lack of dedicated whistleblower legislation. — [Columbia Global Freedom of Expression case page](https://globalfreedomofexpression.columbia.edu/?p=123129); [eucrim](https://eucrim.eu/news/ecthr-grand-chamber-reinforces-protection-of-whistleblowers/); [Oxford Law blog](https://blogs.law.ox.ac.uk/blog-post/2023/02/halet-v-luxembourg-final-act-luxleaks-saga)
- In Bucur and Toma v. Romania (2013) the Court found a violation of Article 10 in the conviction of an employee who exposed unlawful surveillance by the Romanian Intelligence Service, the leading European precedent that intelligence insiders can be protected whistleblowers. — [ECtHR Factsheet](https://www.echr.coe.int/documents/d/echr/FS_Whistleblowers_ENG)
- Council of Europe Recommendation CM/Rec(2014)7 (30 April 2014) states whistleblowers "can contribute to strengthening transparency and democratic accountability" but contains "a significant carve-out for national security interests"; the Parliamentary Assembly asked ministers to draft a binding instrument covering "disclosures of wrongdoings by persons employed in the field of national security and intelligence." — [CoE text](https://rm.coe.int/09000016805c5ea5); [PACE document](https://pace.coe.int/en/files/21651/pdf); [European Law Blog](https://www.europeanlawblog.eu/pub/time-to-reconsider-strasbourgs-whistleblower-case-law/download/pdf)

**Whistleblower vs. leaker vs. spy vs. defector vs. dissident**
- In intelligence tradecraft a "defector" is "an employee of an intelligence service who takes up residence in another country whose spies are not friends" (John Schindler); Peter Oleson treats Snowden's acceptance of Russian citizenship as the sign "that he is a defector rather than a whistleblower." — [AFIO Intelligencer, Winter/Spring 2023](https://archive.afio.com/publications/OLESON_Snowden_Finally_Revealed_AFIO_Intelligencer_WinterSpring_2023_Vol28_No1.pdf)
- A "defector in place" remains inside while working against their government; the physical act of defection "is usually in a manner which violates the laws of the nation... from which the person is seeking to depart." — [Wikipedia, "Defection"](https://Www.Wikipedia.org/wiki/Defected)
- The Philip Agee case shows self-description and recipient judgment diverging: "Although Agee insisted he was a whistle-blower... the KGB viewed him as a conventional spy." Treating press disclosure as equivalent to handing information to adversaries "forecloses on the crucial distinction between a whistleblower and a spy." — [City Journal, "A Tale of Two Defectors"](https://city-journal.org/article/a-tale-of-two-defectors); [Digby's Blog](https://digbysblog.net/?p=21927)
- "Whistleblower, leaker and dissident are often used interchangeably, yet each signals different motives and consequences." — [Melina Dobson, "Rethinking US insider disclosures" (Centrum Balticum)](https://centrumbalticum.org/melina-j-dobson-rethinking-us-insider-disclosures)
- US legal commentary: "Standard whistleblower laws, which protect government workers who expose wrongdoing from retaliation, don't apply to intelligence agency workers. A separate law applies to them." — [NBC, "Is NSA leaker a whistleblower? Legally speaking, no"](https://www.nbcdfw.com/news/national-international/natl-is-nsa-leaker-a-whistleblower-legally-speaking-no/1971084/?amp=1)

### Inferences
- A workable analytical grid for the paper has two independent axes. Axis 1 (content/channel/motive) determines whether an act is whistleblowing: evidence of illegality or grave abuse (Sagar 1-2; Near & Miceli), proportionate and limited disclosure (Sagar 3-4; Tshwane; Guja), good faith and public interest (Guja; Stanger), and whether effective internal channels existed (Tshwane; Guja). Axis 2 (allegiance/recipient) determines whether the person is also a defector or spy: did they switch sides, and did a foreign service rather than the public receive the information. Middle Eastern cases frequently score "yes" on both axes (e.g., defectors who later testify publicly), which is why the labels are contested.
- The Tshwane "internal channels first, provided an effective mechanism exists" clause is the hinge for the region: where no effective internal mechanism exists (most of the states surveyed below), Tshwane logic protects direct public disclosure, whereas US/Israeli law does not.
- Stanger's and Sagar's exclusion of pure policy disagreement cuts against classifying conscientious refusal (e.g., the 2014 Unit 8200 letter) as whistleblowing unless specific illegality is alleged; the signatories' allegation that intelligence was used to blackmail Palestinian civilians is the element that brings the letter closer to the whistleblowing category (see KQ2).

## Key Question 2: Israel — laws and institutions governing security-sector whistleblowing, how they are applied, and press-freedom standing

### Takeaway
Israel is the regional outlier that has both a genuine (if partial) whistleblower-protection architecture (the 1997 Protection of Workers Law and State Comptroller/Ombudsman protection orders) and a security-secrecy regime (Penal Law ss. 111-113, the Military Censor under the 1945 Defence (Emergency) Regulations, and court gag orders) that treats unauthorized security disclosures as espionage regardless of motive. Its classic whistleblowers (Vanunu, Kamm) were therefore prosecuted under espionage provisions, and the civilian protections appear never to have been designed for classified-information disclosures. Israel's press-freedom standing has fallen from 97th-ish "problematic" to 116th "difficult" (RSF 2026), and CIVICUS downgraded its civic space to "Repressed" in December 2025.

### Cited Findings

**Penal Law 5737-1977, sections 111-113 (espionage and "secret information")**
- Section 113(b): a person who "delivered secret information, without being authorized to do so and thereby intended to injure national security... is liable to life imprisonment." Section 113(c): obtaining, collecting, preparing, recording or keeping secret information without authorization carries seven years, or fifteen with intent to injure national security. "Secret information" includes information "which national security requires be kept secret" or categories the Government has declared secret by order published in Reshumot with approval of the Knesset Foreign Affairs and Defense Committee. — [Israel Democracy Institute / iCenter handout: Sections 111-113 of Israel's Penal Code](https://idi.theicenter.org/sites/default/files/Sections%20111-113%20of%20Israel%e2%80%99s%20Penal%20Code_0/index.pdf)
- Application to Anat Kamm: arrested December 2009 by the Shin Bet; indicted 14 January 2010 for aggravated espionage; February 2011 plea deal dropping "intent to harm state security"; sentenced 30 October 2011 to 54 months (prosecutors sought 15 years); Supreme Court reduced the sentence to 3.5 years on 31 December 2012. Kamm said she wanted to expose "war crimes"; judges accused her of "cynically exploiting her position." — [+972 timeline](https://www.972mag.com/timeline-the-anat-kamm-affair/26542/); [Ahram Online](https://english.ahram.org.eg/News/25493.aspx); [JPost, early release](https://www.jpost.com/national-news/anat-kamm-wins-appeal-for-early-release-338463); [IDI/iCenter case study handout](https://idi.theicenter.org/sites/default/files/Handout%204a-%20Case%20Study-%20Anat%20Kamm%20and%20Uri%20Blau_0/index.pdf)
- Critique: legal scholar Mordechai Kremnitzer "decried as draconian the fact that even the first charge falls under the Espionage Law"; commentators warned that if the journalist Uri Blau were charged, "any reporter in Israel who has seen or held a secret document could face indictment." Blau was indicted May 2012 and received four months' community service in a plea bargain. — [Index on Censorship, Anat Kamm tag](https://www.indexoncensorship.org/tag/anat-kamm); [+972](https://www.972mag.com/idf-whistleblower-anat-kamm-to-be-sentenced-sunday/26658/)
- A proposed amendment to distinguish espionage with intent to harm Israel from transfers of classified information without malicious intent was pending in 2012; Kamm petitioned the High Court alleging the committee chair had frozen it. I found no confirmation that it was ever enacted. — [Index on Censorship](https://www.indexoncensorship.org/tag/anat-kamm)
- For soldiers, Article 57 of the Military Justice Law 1955 penalizes unauthorized passing of classified information. — [Zulat position paper on the "Feldstein Law" (2025)](https://en.zulat.org.il/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/Feldstein-Law-Blanket-Immunity-for-Political-Use-of-Classified-Information.pdf)

**The Military Censor (Regulation 87, Defence (Emergency) Regulations 1945) and the Schnitzer test**
- HCJ 680/88 Schnitzer v. Chief Military Censor (decided 10 January 1989): the censor may ban publication only where there is a "near certainty that real damage will be caused to the security of the state"; the Mandate-era regulations are part of Israeli law but must be interpreted in line with democratic values and freedom of the press. The court lifted the ban on an article criticizing the Mossad director. — [Cardozo Versa translation](https://versa.cardozo.yu.edu/node/327); [Quimbee case brief](https://www.quimbee.com/cases/schnitzer-v-chief-military-censor)
- Israeli outlets must submit anything touching "security issues" for pre-publication review; outlets may not disclose that the censor intervened. — [The New Arab explainer](https://www.newarab.com/news/what-israels-military-censor-restricting-war-coverage)
- Scale: in 2024 the censor fully banned 1,635 articles and partially redacted 6,265 more, out of 20,770 submissions (intervening in 38%), the highest in +972's data series since 2011; 2023 saw 613 full bans and 2,703 partial redactions. — [+972, "Breaking new records... 2024"](https://www.972mag.com/israeli-military-censor-media-2024/); [+972, 2023 figures](https://www.972mag.com/israeli-military-censor-media-2023/)
- Already in 1992 Haaretz's editor argued the censor had gone beyond what Schnitzer allowed. — [Christian Science Monitor, 25 Nov 1992](https://www.csmonitor.com/1992/1125/25013.html)

**Court gag orders**
- Noa Landau (Reuters Institute) found gag-order requests rose from 186 (2011) to 231 (2015) and roughly tripled over 15 years; courts "tend to grant these requests without hearing the other side"; "No state body actually monitors them"; the Shin Bet can request them and "is not bound by the Freedom of Information Act." — [Reuters Institute, "State vs Press: the rise of gag orders in Israel"](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/our-research/state-vs-press-rise-gag-orders-israel); [+972 summary](https://www.972mag.com/gag-orders-in-israel-have-tripled-over-last-15-years/)
- Shuki Tausig: gag orders "have replaced the IDF Censor as the establishment's main tool" and courts "usually serve as a rubber stamp... especially in cases when security is somehow involved." — [+972](https://www.972mag.com/the-state-has-a-surefire-way-of-keeping-israelis-in-the-dark/)
- Kremnitzer (IDI) called Israel's handling of suspected security offenses "draconian": "By isolating the suspect and preventing any public debate... the authorities could more easily press the suspect to arrive at a plea bargain." — [IDI/iCenter, "Debate in Israel on Gag Order in Security Leak Case" (April 2010)](https://idi.theicenter.org/sites/default/files/Debate%20in%20Israel%20on%20Gag%20Order%20in%20Security%20Leak%20Case%20%20(April%202010)/index.pdf)
- IDI's Tehilla Shwartz Altshuler objected to the censor enforcing court gag orders: gag orders restrict information about new investigations and are meant to be enforced by police via contempt, whereas "Military censorship... is imposed in emergency situations when there is near certain danger to security"; the arrangement is "not appropriate in a democracy." — [IDI](https://idi.org.il//en.idi.org.il/articles/6314)
- The Prisoner X (Ben Zygier) affair (2010 death; exposed by ABC Australia in February 2013) showed the limits: the PMO summoned editors to maintain a blackout, which "backfired" as Israelis read foreign coverage; the attorney-general's office said the gag order was "vital for preventing a serious breach of the state's security." — [Christian Science Monitor, 15 Feb 2013](https://www.csmonitor.com/World/Middle-East/2013/0215/Prisoner-X-case-strains-Israelis-longtime-acceptance-of-censorship); [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/world/controversy-erupts-two-continents-over-israels-prisoner-x-flna1c8357412)

**Protection of Workers (Exposure of Offenses and of Harm to Integrity or to Proper Administration) Law 5757-1997**
- Enacted 1997, amended three times; "civilly and criminally forbids employers from retaliating against employees for whistleblowing"; cases go to the specialized Labor Courts, which may award compensatory and punitive damages, void a dismissal or order transfer to "another appropriate position." Weaknesses identified: a 12-month limitation period "will often preclude the possibility of an amicable out-of-court settlement," and most complaints to the Labor Ministry are closed. — [Global Anticorruption Blog, "Two Legal Changes Which Would Bolster Israel's Protection of Whistleblowers" (July 2020)](https://globalanticorruptionblog.com/2020/07/13/two-legal-changes-which-would-bolster-israels-protection-of-whistleblowers/)
- Criminal penalty for harming a whistleblower: up to three years' imprisonment or a fine of up to NIS 75,300. — [Jerusalem Post, Teva case](https://m.jpost.com/israel-news/article-839758)

**State Comptroller and Ombudsman protection orders (State Comptroller Law ss. 45A-45E)**
- Sections 45A-45C empower the Ombudsman to investigate complaints by public employees "victimized for exposing acts of corruption," a serious violation of law or a serious breach of proper administration; a complaint must satisfy all conditions in s. 45A before a protection order under s. 45C can issue. Remedies: temporary protection order, reinstatement, transfer, special compensation, revocation of dismissal; the aim is "enforcement of work relations existing prior to the complaint." — [State Comptroller, Protection of Whistleblowers brochure](https://www.mevaker.gov.il/En/Ombudsman/Guidecomplainant/Documents/Protection-of-Whistleblowers-Brochure.pdf); [State Comptroller Ombudsman page](https://www.mevaker.gov.il/En/Ombudsman/NTZAbout/Pages/NTZDefense.aspx); [Brookdale Institute evaluation](https://brookdale.jdc.org.il/en/publication/the-effectiveness-of-the-protection-provided-to-whistleblowers-by-the-ombudsman/)
- The Ombudsman has issued protection orders in the nuclear establishment: a 2014 order protected an Israel Atomic Energy Commission whistleblower. — [Jerusalem Post](https://www.jpost.com/breaking-news/state-ombudsman-orders-protection-of-atomic-agency-whistleblower-378182)
- Institutional fragility (2026): Israel has had no State Comptroller since Matanyahu Englman's term expired on 5 July 2026; the High Court voided the June 2026 Knesset election of Michael Rabello (Netanyahu's lawyer) over a ballot-secrecy breach and ordered a re-vote; with no authorized head, a fired public employee seeking emergency protection "found the office had no authorized head to consider his case" and went to the labor court. — [Times of Israel](https://www.timesofisrael.com/netanyahu-forces-through-election-of-his-lawyer-as-state-comptroller-amid-tainted-vote/); [The Jewish Independent](https://thejewishindependent.com.au/netanyahu-rabello-state-comptroller/); [Jerusalem Post](https://jpost.com/israel-election-2026/article-907794)

**Current legislative debates touching security whistleblowing**
- A "Bill on Amendment of Penal Code (Protection of Whistleblowers in Corruption and Security Contexts) 2024" was tabled in the Knesset on 25 November 2024 alongside the so-called "Feldstein Law," which would (a) allow classified information to be passed directly to the Prime Minister and certain ministers and (b) extend immunity to police and "employees of security agencies such as the Shin Bet, Mossad, and the IDF" who share secret documents with the PM's or Defence Minister's office without authorization. It passed a preliminary reading 59-52 on 4 December 2024. Zulat calls it "Blanket Immunity for Political Use of Classified Information" and warns it "could undermine the principle of nonpartisanship and lead to severe politicization in the Shin Bet, the Mossad, the IDF." IDF spokesman Daniel Hagari called it "dangerous"; MK Gadi Eisenkot called it "a transparent personal law." — [Zulat position paper](https://en.zulat.org.il/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/Feldstein-Law-Blanket-Immunity-for-Political-Use-of-Classified-Information.pdf); [Zulat summary](https://en.zulat.org.il/feldstein-law-blanket-immunity-for-political-use-of-classified-information/); [Jerusalem Post](https://jpost.com/israel-news/article-831975)

**Scholarly treatment of Vanunu and the Unit 8200 refusers (as framing illustrations)**
- Yoel Cohen, The Whistleblower of Dimona (2003) and Whistleblowers and the Bomb (Pluto, 2005), drawing on "thousands of pages of court transcripts," portrays Vanunu as "a complicated and even tragic figure" and uses the case "to examine democracy in Israel, including what he calls 'excessive secrecy.'" Vanunu was convicted of espionage and treason and sentenced to 18 years. — [Pluto Press](https://www.plutobooks.com/product/whistleblowers-and-the-bomb/); [Lynne Rienner](https://rienner.com/title/The_Whistleblower_of_Dimona_Israel_Vanunu_and_the_Bomb); [Jerusalem Post review, "Traitor or tragic figure"](https://m.jpost.com/arts-and-culture/books/traitor-or-tragic-figure)
- Nobel laureate Joseph Rotblat called Vanunu "a sincere, whistle-blower, who attempted to warn the world of a potential source of danger." — [The Scientist](https://www.the-scientist.com/scientists-whistle-blowers-and-the-vanunu-affair-63081)
- Avner Cohen (The Worst-Kept Secret, Columbia UP 2010) argues Israel's nuclear opacity (amimut) "remains incompatible with liberal democracy, relies on secrecy and opacity, and infringes on the public right to know"; "total secrecy stifles open debate and undermines Israeli democracy." — [Arms Control Today review](https://www.armscontrol.org/act/2010-11/wrestling-nuclear-opacity); [Arms Control Wonk](https://www.armscontrolwonk.com/archive/502728/israel-between-dimona-and-democracy/)
- Unit 8200 letter (published in Yedioth Ahronoth and The Guardian, 12 September 2014, 43 signatories): Bruce Riedel (Brookings) saw it as significant for breaking taboos about discussing intelligence openly, knew of no earlier refusal from the signals-intelligence service, but judged it less consequential than earlier pilot/ground-troop refusals because 8200 "is not a front-line unit." The IDF dismissed signatories from reserve duty as "a grave breach of the military code of conduct"; the IDF spokesman said they "used military service to express a political opinion"; +972 called them "whistle-blowers" whose charge that intelligence was used to blackmail innocent Palestinians went unanswered. — [Brookings, Riedel](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/israeli-intelligence-veterans-refusal-letter/); [Institute for Palestine Studies document](https://www.palestine-studies.org/ar/node/188335); [+972, dismissal](https://www.972mag.com/idf-commander-dismisses-unit-8200-refuseniks/101863/); [Ynet](https://www.ynetnews.com/article/4619538)

**Index standings**
- RSF World Press Freedom Index: 2024, 101st ("problematic" to "difficult"; overtaken by Qatar as regional leader); 2025, 112th (down 11); 2026, 116th (down 4, attributed to the Gaza war). — [RSF 2024 MENA ranking](https://rsf.org/en/classement/2024/middle-east-north-africa); [RSF MENA regional analysis](https://rsf.org/en/analyse_regionale/1344); [RSF MENA analysis (newer)](https://rsf.org/en/analyse_regionale/1499)
- Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2025: Israel 73/100, Free (political rights 34/40, civil liberties 39/60), down from 74; score excludes the West Bank and Gaza. IDI describes "a pattern of continuing decline." — [ecoi.net mirror of FIW 2025 Israel](https://www.ecoi.net/en/document/2129050.html); [IDI analysis](https://en.idi.org.il/articles/58636)
- CIVICUS Monitor downgraded Israel from "Obstructed" to "Repressed" in December 2025 (People Power Under Attack 2025), citing violent policing of protests and legislation threatening rights work. — [CIVICUS press release](https://monitor.civicus.org/press_release/2025/israel/)

### Inferences
- Israel's framework separates "integrity" whistleblowing (corruption, maladministration), which is protected, from "security" whistleblowing, which is criminalized: the 1997 Law and ss. 45A-45C protect against employer retaliation but do not create any defence to a s. 113 prosecution. This is why Israel produces recognizable whistleblowers who are nonetheless convicted as spies (Vanunu, Kamm): the system has a public-interest ethic without a public-interest defence.
- The Schnitzer "near certainty" standard is, on paper, closer to Tshwane Principle 3 than any other regional rule; the 2024 censorship figures and the gag-order data suggest the gap between doctrine and practice widens sharply in wartime.
- The 2024-25 Feldstein legislation inverts the whistleblower logic: it would immunize leaks upward to political principals rather than outward to the public, which Zulat and legal critics read as politicizing classified information rather than protecting conscience-driven disclosure.

### Gaps
- I found no source stating whether IDF soldiers, Shin Bet or Mossad employees are formally covered by or excluded from the 1997 Law or Ombudsman protection orders; the brochure excerpts are silent. The student should check the law's text and the Ombudsman's annual reports.
- Status of the 2012 espionage-law amendment and of the 2024 "Protection of Whistleblowers in Corruption and Security Contexts" bill and the Feldstein bills after the preliminary reading is unverified.
- Current (2026) gag-order statistics were not found; the Landau data end in 2015.

## Key Question 3: Iran — espionage and "propaganda" provisions, cyber and "Protection" laws, the Press Law, absence of a whistleblower statute, and the fate of insiders

### Takeaway
Iran has no whistleblower statute for the security sector; its only "informant protection" provisions sit in a 2011 anti-corruption law (Article 17: identity confidentiality) and apply to economic crimes. Disclosure of state secrets is punished under Islamic Penal Code Articles 501/505 (one to ten years), "propaganda against the system" under Article 500, and, since the October 2025 espionage law, cooperation with Israel/US can be charged as "corruption on earth" (Article 286) with death. The 2009 Computer Crimes Law and the 2021-22 "User Protection" bill add internet controls. Human-rights groups document that even victims and witnesses of abuses are punished, and that leaks from inside the system are met with arrests of suspected sources rather than investigation of the content.

### Cited Findings

**Islamic Penal Code provisions**
- Article 500: "Anyone who, in any form, engages in propaganda activities against the system of the Islamic Republic of Iran or in favour of groups and organizations opposed to the system, will be sentenced to imprisonment ranging from three months to one year." Amended February 2021 (signed by Rouhani) to add "deviant education and proselytizing activity" contradicting Islam, with up to five years. Impact Iran notes the propaganda provisions "have been interpreted to include peaceful protests." — [International Christian Concern on 2021 amendments](https://persecution.org/2021/03/04/iran-updates-strict-penal-code-amidst-christian-detainments/); [Impact Iran assessment](https://impactiran.uwazi.io/en/entity/o4qzts3pj0g?page=1)
- Espionage is never defined; "there is no consensus amongst legal experts and jurists as to which articles addresses espionage." Article 501: transferring state plans, secrets or policy documents to unauthorized persons, or disclosing them in a manner amounting to espionage, carries one to ten years; Article 505 covers collecting classified material to undermine national security; Article 508 covers collaboration with hostile states. — [Abdorrahman Boroumand Center](https://www.iranrights.org/library/document/5263/); [IranWire defence-lawyer analysis](https://iranwire.com/en/politics/61795)
- HRW on the 2012-13 code revision: "These amendments do little to address penal code provisions that allow the government to jail, torture, and execute people who criticize the government." — [HRW, "Iran: Proposed Penal Code Deeply Flawed" (Aug 2012)](https://www.hrw.org/news/2012/08/29/iran-proposed-penal-code-deeply-flawed)
- Article 286 (2013 code) made "corruption on earth" (efsad fel-arz) an independent capital offence; Iran Human Rights: "due to their vague definitions, the charges of moharebeh, efsad-fil-arz and baghy are used for a wide range of offences." — [Iran Human Rights](https://iranhr.net/en/print/5/5414/); [Magiran legal review](https://magiran.com/paper/1699003/?lang=en)

**The 2025 espionage law**
- The "Law on Intensifying Punishment for Espionage and Collaboration with Israel" was fast-tracked in June 2025 (after the Israel-Iran war), returned by the Guardian Council in July for ambiguities, adopted by the Majles on 28 September 2025 and promulgated on 15 October 2025; it classifies espionage or intelligence cooperation with Israel and the US as "corruption on earth" and reaches intelligence sharing, financial transactions including crypto, and equipment such as Starlink. — [Iran Human Rights, "Increased Repression Under Shadow of War"](https://iranhr.net/en/print/5/7693/); [Iran International, 700 arrests (June 2025)](https://www.iranintl.com/en/202506250530); [WJWC report](https://wjwc.org/reports/irans-espionage-law-a-dissent-silencing-tool)
- Amnesty (June 2025): the bill lets authorities impose death sentences for speech, and espionage does not meet the "most serious crimes" threshold for capital punishment; UN High Commissioner Volker Türk: "This bill dangerously broadens the scope of capital punishment for espionage, and I call for it to be rescinded." — [Amnesty International](https://www.amnesty.org/en/latest/news/2025/06/iran-growing-fears-over-torture-and-executions-of-individuals-accused-of-espionage-for-israel/); [Al-Monitor](https://www.al-monitor.com/index%2Ephp/originals/2025/06/iran-hangs-three-more-accused-spying-fears-grow-swede)
- Judiciary spokesman Asghar Jahangir: 3,121 people prosecuted under the law, 2,406 detained, 1,000 indictments; 43% charged with "cooperation in political, cultural, media, and propaganda activities benefiting Israel," only 7% with direct collaboration. — [HRANA](https://www.en-hrana.org/?p=59359)
- Executions: at least 13 people executed for espionage for Israel in 2025 and at least 14 for espionage in 2026 (to 3 August); IHR documents coerced confessions. Amnesty: over 1,000 executions in 2025, with capital punishment intensified "under the guise of national security." — [IHR, Aug 2026](https://iranhr.net/en/print/5/8946/); [Amnesty, Sept 2025](https://www.amnesty.org/en/latest/news/2025/09/iran-over-1000-people-executed-as-authorities-step-up-horrifying-assault-on-right-to-life)

**Cyber and media laws**
- Computer Crimes Law (approved 2009; some sources say 2010/2011): 56 articles; ARTICLE 19 concluded it "flagrantly violates international human rights law" and called for repeal; offences on "public morality and chastity" and "dissemination of lies" are broad enough to capture lawful expression; Article 21 imposes filtering liability on ISPs; Articles 22-23 create the Committee Charged with Determining Offensive Content. — [ARTICLE 19 analysis (2012)](https://www.article19.org/data/files/medialibrary/2921/12-01-30-FINAL-iran-WEB%5B4%5D.pdf); [ARTICLE 19, "Computer Crimes in Iran: Risky Online Behaviour"](https://www.article19.org/resources/computer-crimes-in-iran-risky-online-behaviour/); [IHRDC English translation](https://iranhrdc.org/islamic-republic-of-iran-computer-crimes-law/)
- "User Protection Bill" / Regulatory System for Cyberspace Services Bill: ratified in a joint parliamentary committee 18-1 on 22 February 2022 (vote revoked next day on procedural grounds); would place international gateways under a Secure Gateway Taskforce including the Armed Forces General Staff, IRGC Intelligence Organization and Ministry of Intelligence; 55+ groups including Amnesty, HRW and ARTICLE 19 called it "draconian"; UN experts said it gives the military "extensive control over infrastructure that connects Iran to the global internet." — [Amnesty joint statement, 2022](https://www.amnesty.org/en/documents/mde13/5361/2022/en/); [ARTICLE 19](https://www.article19.org/resources/iran-human-rights-groups-sound-alarm-against-draconian-internet-bill/); [Arab News on UN experts](https://www.arabnews.com/node/2034071/middle-east); [Filterwatch](https://filter.watch/english/2022/03/29/policy-monitory-february-2022/)
- Press Law 1986: rests on Constitution Article 24 (press freedom conditional on not violating Islamic principles); sets twelve grounds for censorship including "publishing heretical articles"; Impact Iran says it "significantly obstruct[s] the right to freedom of expression, in particular for the media." — [Impact Iran](https://impactiran.uwazi.io/en/entity/mjncmir35p?page=1); [WIPO Lex text](https://wipo.int/wipolex/zh/legislation/details/14370)

**Whistleblower provisions (anti-corruption only)**
- Law on Promotion of Administrative Integrity and Combating Corruption (approved by the Expediency Council 7 Aban 1390 / 29 Oct 2011 as a three-year trial; made permanent 2019). Article 17 lists "protective measures" beginning with non-disclosure of the identity, family details and residence of informants, except where the trial judge deems disclosure necessary for the accused's defence; Article 13 imposes a mandatory duty on officials to report bribery, embezzlement and fraud, with penalties under Penal Code Article 606 for failure. — [Wikihoghoogh, Article 17 (Persian)](https://wikihoghoogh.net/wiki/%D9%85%D8%A7%D8%AF%D9%87_%DB%B1%DB%B7_%D9%82%D8%A7%D9%86%D9%88%D9%86_%D8%A7%D8%B1%D8%AA%D9%82%D8%A7%D8%A1_%D8%B3%D9%84%D8%A7%D9%85%D8%AA_%D9%86%D8%B8%D8%A7%D9%85_%D8%A7%D8%AF%D8%A7%D8%B1%DB%8C_%D9%88_%D9%85%D9%82%D8%A7%D8%A8%D9%84%D9%87_%D8%A8%D8%A7_%D9%81%D8%B3%D8%A7%D8%AF); [HSE Anti-Corruption Portal, Iran](https://anticor.hse.ru/en/main/country/Iran)
- A 2019 bill "Support for Whistleblowers of Economic and Administrative Corruption" was signed by 94 MPs. — [Al-Monitor, Feb 2019](https://al-monitor.com/originals/2019/02/iran-corruption-whistleblowers-bill-lawmakers-mps-rouhani.html)
- Iranian academic critique: the system's "fundamental challenge is a lack of measures and criteria for determining the scope of the whistleblower and the method of protecting them." — [Islamic Azad University Yazd](https://imr.yazd.iau.ir/article_710045.html?lang=en)
- The judiciary has warned domestic media over corruption coverage. — [Al-Monitor, Oct 2014](https://al-monitor.com/originals/2014/10/iran-judiciary-warns-media-corruption-cases.html)

**What happens to insiders and witnesses**
- Amnesty (2009, post-election abuses): authorities had "further punish[ed] those victims and witnesses who courageously reported" abuses; Amnesty urged the Supreme Leader to protect rape and torture victims "against reprisals." — [Amnesty USA, Dec 2009](https://www.amnestyusa.org/press-releases/iran-amnesty-international-says-iran-focused-on-covering-up-horrific-abuses-committed-during-post-election-period/); [Amnesty UK, "Rape and other torture victims at renewed risk"](https://www.amnesty.org.uk/knowledge-hub/all-resources/iran-rape-and-other-torture-victims-renewed-risk/)
- Amnesty (Dec 2023) cited a leaked October 2022 prosecutor's document advising that a rape complaint against two IRGC agents be classified "completely secret" and allowed to "close over time." — [Amnesty UK](https://www.amnesty.org.uk/knowledge-hub/all-resources/iran-rape-and-other-torture-victims-renewed-risk/)
- Leaks are met with source hunts: in March 2023 the intelligence unit of Khamenei's office arrested three people for disclosing documents of an IRGC commanders' meeting; in December 2022 the deputy head of IRGC-linked Fars News was arrested for "forging briefing reports" after a hacktivist release. — [Iran International, March 2023](https://www.iranintl.com/en/202303234847); [Iran International, Dec 2022](https://www.iranintl.com/en/202212064502)
- Amnesty's September 2026 report on the 2022 "Woman, Life, Freedom" crackdown (c. 1,000+ pages) drew on 270 witness interviews, 2,000+ videos and "leaked official Iranian government documents." — [Iran International](https://www.iranintl.com/en/202609163078)

**Index standings**
- RSF: 176th in both 2024 and 2025; 177th in 2026. — [Iran International on RSF 2024](https://iranintl.com/en/202405038337); [RSF MENA analysis](https://rsf.org/en/analyse_regionale/1344)
- Freedom House FIW 2025: 11/100, Not Free (10/100 in FIW 2026). — [ecoi.net FIW 2026 Iran (showing prior-year score)](https://www.ecoi.net/en/document/2138123.html)
- CIVICUS Monitor: "Closed" since the Monitor's 2016 launch. — [Gulf Centre for Human Rights on CIVICUS launch](https://www.gc4hr.org/worldwide-attack-on-fundamental-civic-freedoms-laid-bare-by-new-online-tool-that-rates-civic-space-and-documents-rights-violations/)

### Inferences
- Iran's legal architecture leaves no category of "protected disclosure" for security matters: the same facts can be charged as propaganda (Art. 500), disclosure of secrets (Art. 501/505), or, if a foreign link is alleged, as capital "corruption on earth" (Art. 286 via the 2025 law). The 43% share of 2025-26 prosecutions for "media and propaganda activities" shows the law functions against speech rather than classic espionage.
- The only exposures of Iranian security-service conduct that reach the public therefore come via hacktivist dumps, defectors abroad, or victims' testimony gathered by NGOs, consistent with the regional pattern discussed in KQ6.

### Gaps
- I could not confirm whether the 2013 penal-code revision renumbered Articles 500-508 (most sources cite the 1996 Ta'zirat numbering).
- Fate of the "User Protection Bill" after March 2022 is not documented in the sources found.
- Scope of Article 17 of the 2011 anti-corruption law (whether it covers only informants and whether any anti-retaliation remedy exists) could not be verified from the partial Persian text; an English translation was not found.
- No HRW/Amnesty document was found that specifically profiles a security-service insider punished for exposing abuses (as opposed to victims/witnesses or suspected sources).

## Key Question 4: Gulf monarchies — counterterrorism and cybercrime laws, whistleblower channels and whether they reach security matters, and index standings

### Takeaway
All four Gulf states examined have cybercrime and/or counterterrorism laws that human-rights bodies describe as vague enough to criminalize criticism of security services ("harming public order," "false news," "undermining national unity"), and none has a whistleblower law that reaches security matters. Saudi Arabia's 2024 Law on the Protection of Whistleblowers, Witnesses, Experts and Victims and the UAE's Dubai Law No. 4 of 2016 are anti-corruption and criminal-justice instruments administered by the prosecution and security ministries themselves. Freedom House rates all four Not Free (Saudi 9, Bahrain 12, UAE 18, Qatar 25 out of 100), and CIVICUS rates Saudi Arabia, the UAE and Bahrain "Closed."

### Cited Findings

**Saudi Arabia**
- Anti-Cybercrime Law (2007), Article 6: up to five years and a 3 million riyal fine for "producing something that harms public order, religious values, public morals, the sanctity of private life, or authoring, sending, or storing it via an information network"; HRW (Nov 2014): "Saudi prosecutors and judges are using vague provisions of a 2007 anti-cybercrime law to charge and try Saudi citizens for peaceful tweets." — [HRW, "Saudi Arabia: Assault on Online Expression"](https://www.hrw.org/news/2014/11/22/saudi-arabia-assault-online-expression)
- Law on Combating Terrorism Crimes and its Financing (promulgated November 2017, replacing the 2014 law): "vague and overly broad definitions of acts of terrorism, in some cases punishable by death"; 5-10 years for portraying the king or crown prince "in a manner that brings religion or justice into disrepute"; "criminalizes a wide range of peaceful acts that bear no relation to terrorism" (HRW). Alkarama: the law "does not consider the use of violence as a sine qua non element." ADHRB (2024): the law "remains taunted by extreme repression of freedom of expression." — [HRW, "Saudi Arabia: New Counterterrorism Law Enables Abuse" (23 Nov 2017)](https://www.hrw.org/news/2017/11/23/saudi-arabia-new-counterterrorism-law-enables-abuse); [Alkarama](https://www.alkarama.org/en/node/23443); [ADHRB 2017](https://www.adhrb.org/2017/11/new-saudi-counter-terrorism-law-fails-to-meet-international-standards/); [ADHRB 2024](https://www.adhrb.org/2024/03/analysing-the-upr-of-saudi-arabia-new-recommendations-and-foreseeable-developments-counter-terrorism-legislation-part-three/)
- Whistleblower channels: a 2018 royal order barred retaliation against employees reporting graft and required Nazaha to report agencies that threaten them; Nazaha "protects everyone offering information, provided they observe secrecy of their reports." — [Khaleej Times via PressReader, May 2018](https://www.pressreader.com/uae/khaleej-times/20180507/282316795667229); [Gulf News](https://gulfnews.com/world/gulf/saudi/saudi-arabia-anti-corruption-watchdog-pledges-confidentiality-protection-to-whistleblowers-1.76507437)
- Law on the Protection of Whistleblowers, Witnesses, Experts and Victims: approved by the Council of Ministers 13 February 2024 (Royal Decree M/148), gazetted 1 March 2024, in force 28 June 2024; implementing regulation published 12 June 2026. Coverage is limited to "major crimes warranting arrest" (corruption, bribery, money laundering, forgery). A new centre in the Public Prosecution handles protection requests and implements measures "with the Interior Ministry and the Presidency of State Security"; the Prosecutor-General may order protection for 30 days without a request. Measures: identity concealment, altered voice/image testimony. Harmful disclosure of a protected person's identity: up to one year and SR200,000. — [Gulf News, June 2024](https://gulfnews.com/world/gulf/saudi/saudi-arabia-new-law-for-whistleblowers-protection-takes-effect-hefty-fines-and-jail-terms-imposed-1.1719653652633); [HSE Anti-Corruption Portal](https://anticor.hse.ru/en/main/news_page/saudi_arabia_adopts_a_law_on_protection_of_whistleblowers); [Lexis Middle East](https://www.lexis.ae/?p=22877); [Safecall summary](https://www.safecall.co.uk/saudi-arabia-law-on-the-protection-of-whistleblowers-witnesses-experts-and-victims/); [DLA Piper, March 2024](https://www.dlapiper.com/insights/publications/2024/03/navigating-the-legal-landscape-of-whistleblowing-in-ksa)
- A Government Accountability Project commentary (URL dated November 2017; its headline references the Khashoggi murder of October 2018, so it was evidently updated) argued that Saudi journalists and human-rights activists lacked legal protection and that a 2011 royal decree criminalized criticism of government officials: "New whistleblower protection laws prove toothless." — [GAP](https://whistleblower.org/uncategorized/112017-khashoggi-murder-proves-actions-speak-louder-words-saudi-arabia/)
- Index standings: RSF 166th (2024), 162nd (2025), 176th (2026, entering the bottom ten "for the first time," linked to the execution of journalist Turki al-Jasser); Freedom House FIW 2025 9/100 (PR 1/40, CL 8/60), regime "depends on widespread surveillance and the punishment of dissent," at least 338 executions in 2024. — [RSF MENA analysis](https://rsf.org/en/analyse_regionale/1499); [ecoi.net FIW 2025 Saudi Arabia](https://www.ecoi.net/en/document/2129052.html)

**United Arab Emirates**
- Federal Law No. 7 of 2014 on Combating Terrorism Offences (August 2014): HRW said it allows "any act that courts deem to have antagonized the state, stirred panic, or undermined national unity to be designated as terrorism," with the death penalty for undermining "national unity or social peace," neither defined; Sarah Leah Whitson: "you're with us or you're a terrorist." Used alongside the 2012 cybercrime law against academic Nasser bin Ghaith for tweets; HRW (2019) documented indefinite "counselling" detention at Al-Razeen after sentences ended; in July 2024, 43 of 84 defendants in a mass trial received life sentences on terrorism charges. — [HRW, "UAE: Terrorism Law Threatens Lives, Liberty" (Dec 2014)](https://www.hrw.org/news/2014/12/04/uae-terrorism-law-threatens-lives-liberty); [HRW World Report 2015](https://www.hrw.org/world-report/2015/country-chapters/united-arab-emirates); [Al Jazeera on HRW 2019](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2019/7/9/uae-holding-prisoners-after-completion-of-their-sentences-hrw); [ecoi.net FIW 2025 UAE](https://www.ecoi.net/en/document/2129124.html)
- Federal Decree-Law No. 34 of 2021 on Combatting Rumours and Cybercrimes (gazetted 26 Sept 2021, in force 2 Jan 2022, replacing Federal Law 5 of 2012): penalizes breaching government information systems, "disseminating false information," and similar conduct; fines range up to Dh10 million. — [UAE government portal](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/justice-safety-and-the-law/cyber-safety-and-digital-security/law-on-combatting-rumours-and-cybercrimes); [Clyde & Co](https://www.clydeco.com/en/insights/2022/03/uae-updates-cybercrime-law)
- Whistleblower channels: "There is no single, comprehensive federal law safeguarding whistleblowers"; DIFC and ADGM free zones run their own regimes; Dubai Law No. 4 of 2016 (Dubai Economic Security Centre) protects reporters only where the disclosure is true, concerns Dubai's "economic security," and is made to the DESC; Article 47 of the Labour Law bars dismissal for a "serious and truthful complaint." Norton Rose Fulbright (2018): in several Gulf states "whistleblowing may lead to criminal and civil liability for breach of confidentiality and criminal liability for defamation." — [Afridi & Angell, 2023](https://afridi-angell.com/evolving-landscape-for-whistleblower-protection-in-the-uae/); [Pinsent Masons guide](https://pinsentmasons.com/out-law/guides/whistleblowing-protections-in-the-uae); [Mondaq on Dubai Law 4/2016](https://www.mondaq.com/whistleblowing/510046/new-protection-for-whistleblowers-in-dubai); [HSF Kramer, 2025](https://www.hsfkramer.com/de/notes/middle-east/2025-posts/updates-to-uae-whistleblowing-protections); [Norton Rose Fulbright](https://nortonrosefulbright.com/en/knowledge/publications/25e4ba0a/global-asset-management-quarterly---the-middle-east)
- A 2024 Journal of Money Laundering Control article on UAE whistleblowing recommends awareness programmes "to address the conservative culture that discourages whistleblowing." — [Abu Dhabi University repository](https://repository.adu.ac.ae/items/85342308-04c6-4167-a609-b294d27e6061)
- Index standings: RSF 160th (2024), 164th (2025), 158th (2026); FIW 2025 18/100 (PR 5/40, CL 13/60); CIVICUS "Closed." — [RSF MENA analysis](https://rsf.org/en/analyse_regionale/1344); [ecoi.net FIW 2025 UAE](https://www.ecoi.net/en/document/2129124.html); [GC4HR on CIVICUS ratings](https://www.gc4hr.org/time-is-running-out-civic-space-closing-rapidly-in-the-middle-east/)

**Qatar**
- Law No. 14 of 2014 on Cybercrime Prevention (promulgated 15/16 Sept 2014): Amnesty: authorities "may ban websites that they consider threatening to the 'safety' of the country and punish anyone who posts or shares online content that 'undermines' Qatar's 'social values' or 'general order', though the law fails to define the meaning of these terms"; "a major setback for freedom of expression in Qatar." ### Inferences
- The Gulf states' "whistleblower" instruments are structurally incapable of protecting a security-sector insider: Saudi protection is administered jointly by the Public Prosecution, Interior Ministry and Presidency of State Security, i.e., the bodies whose conduct a security whistleblower would be exposing, and coverage is limited to enumerated economic/criminal offences. The Dubai regime is limited to "economic security" disclosures made to the DESC.
- Because every public exposure of Gulf security services in the last decade has come from foreign contractors (Project Raven), foreign researchers (Citizen Lab), or exiled former officials (al-Jabri), the Gulf cases fit the "defector/outsider" rather than the "domestic whistleblower" pattern; the cybercrime "false news" and counterterrorism "national unity" clauses explain why domestic disclosure is not attempted.

- Bahrain's 2025 RSF rank (c. 157th) and Lebanon's are derived from 2026 year-on-year changes rather than stated directly; verify on rsf.org.

- Whether Qatar or Bahrain has any whistleblower provision (even anti-corruption) was not found.

## Key Question 5: Iraq, Syria, Egypt, Turkey, Jordan, Lebanon — brief survey of whistleblower protections, security overrides and press-freedom status

### Takeaway
Three of the six (Iraq, Jordan, Lebanon) have formal anti-corruption whistleblower laws (Iraq Law 58/2017, Jordan Regulation 62/2014, Lebanon Law 83/2018), all of which channel disclosures exclusively to state anti-corruption bodies and exclude media/NGO disclosure; Egypt and Turkey have none; Syria's post-Assad transition has not yet repealed the 2022 cybercrime law. In every case, counterterrorism, cybercrime or intelligence-service laws impose criminal liability for publishing security information, with the Turkish MIT trucks prosecutions (Dündar 27.5 years in absentia; Berberoğlu 5 years 10 months for "disclosing a state secret") as the paradigm case of a security "leak" prosecuted as espionage.

### Inferences
- The three MENA whistleblower statutes (Iraq, Jordan, Lebanon) share a design that TI's regional guide criticizes: single-channel disclosure to a state commission, no protection for media/NGO disclosure, and no carve-in for security matters. They are therefore irrelevant to intelligence-sector exposure, which by definition concerns the state's own coercive organs.
- The Syrian experience reframes "whistleblowing" as post hoc defector testimony in foreign courts. The Koblenz dilemma (insiders exposing crimes while incriminating themselves, with no witness protection) is the regional analogue of the US "no public-interest defence" problem and should be flagged in the paper's conclusion.
- Turkey is the clearest case of a security "leak" that met Sagar's criteria (documentary evidence of a contested covert policy) being prosecuted as espionage; the appellate finding that a secret already public cannot be the object of espionage is a rare judicial limit, later overridden.

### Gaps
- Lebanon Law 83/2018: no source confirms a textual "national security exception"; the EUAA summary mentions only "vague security provisions." The student should read the UNDP English text directly.
- Egypt's TI "whistleblowing overview" could not be fetched; its date and findings are not quoted here.
- Iraq: status of a broader whistleblower bill after Law 58/2017 is unknown.

## Key Question 6: Comparative and regional scholarship on whistleblowing and "speaking truth to power" inside Middle Eastern security states

### Takeaway
There is no dedicated scholarly literature on intelligence-community whistleblowing in the Middle East; the student must triangulate from (a) anti-corruption whistleblowing studies (Transparency International's MENA guide and country overviews), (b) security-sector-governance scholarship (Sayigh at Carnegie, Abdelfettah in Hikama, Rathmell on Syria's mukhabarat), (c) case-specific works (Yoel Cohen and Avner Cohen on Vanunu/opacity; Riedel on Unit 8200; Justice Info/SJAC on Syrian insider witnesses), and (d) civic-space indices (CIVICUS: MENA is "the most repressed region," 10 Closed/7 Repressed/2 Obstructed out of 19 states). Together they support the pattern that exposures of Gulf and Iranian services come from defectors abroad, hackers and foreign researchers, while Israel produces classic whistleblowers who are prosecuted under espionage law.

### Cited Findings
- Transparency International, Speaking Up Safely: Civil Society Guide to Whistleblowing in the Middle East and North Africa (John Devitt; dated 2015 by Basel Institute, 2016 by TI): maps whistleblowing provisions, "what helps informants feel secure," and advocacy strategies; TI's companion working papers on seven MENA countries (Jordan, Egypt, Yemen, Palestine among them) each review a national framework and propose reforms. TI's regional finding, as summarized by JMCC: "specific whistleblowing provisions are almost entirely absent across the Middle East and North Africa," with clear reporting procedures and anti-retaliation protections missing; in TI's 2016 nine-country MENA survey about a third of respondents feared retaliation if they reported corruption. — [TI publication page](https://transparency.org/en/publications/speaking-up-safely-civil-society-guide-to-whistleblowing-mena); [Basel Institute](https://baselgovernance.org/resources/publications/speaking-safely-civil-society-guide-whistleblowing-middle-east-and-north-africa-region/); [JMCC summary](https://www.jmcc.org/news.aspx?id=864); [TI Jordan overview](https://www.transparency.org/en/publications/jordan-whistleblowing-overview)
- Louay Abdelfettah, "Intelligence and Decision-Makers in the Arab World: Diagnosing the Relationship and Democratic Governance," Hikama 11 (Doha Institute, 2025): across Morocco, Egypt, Tunisia and Algeria after 2011, intelligence agencies "remain largely politicized, lacking independence, impartiality, and effective institutional oversight"; "structural and behavioral changes within intelligence institutions have been minimal"; governance "continues to reflect authoritarian legacies." — [Hikama](https://hikama.dohainstitute.org/en/issue011/Pages/art07.aspx)
- Yezid Sayigh, "Reforming the Arab Security State" (Carnegie Middle East Center, March 2016): democratization "depends crucially on transforming their security sectors"; "A technocratic focus on upgrading skills and operational capability, in the absence of improved governance of the security services, can be easily subverted by anti-reform coalitions"; counterterrorism focus has slowed reform in Egypt and Tunisia. Carnegie's January 2014 Amman conference agenda covered legal protection, codes of conduct and "the role of the media" in security-sector reform. — [Carnegie](https://carnegieendowment.org/posts/2016/03/reforming-the-arab-security-state); [Carnegie 2014 conference](https://carnegieendowment.org/middle-east/events/2014/01/arab-security-sector-restructuring-and-transformation)

### Inferences
- The scholarship supports a three-tier typology for the paper: (1) Israel, a "secrecy-within-democracy" state with contested but real institutions (Supreme Court review, Comptroller, labor-court protections) that produces insider whistleblowers but prosecutes them as spies; (2) Turkey, Jordan, Lebanon, Iraq, hybrid or partly free systems with anti-corruption whistleblower laws that exclude security matters and criminalize security journalism; (3) Iran, the Gulf, Egypt and (until 2024) Syria, "Closed" mukhabarat states where no domestic channel exists and exposure is externalized to defectors, hackers, foreign researchers and foreign courts.
- Sayigh's warning that capability upgrades without governance reform are "easily subverted" explains why Saudi and UAE whistleblower instruments, which upgrade reporting capability inside the prosecution/security apparatus, do not translate into accountability for the security services.

## Key Question 7: How US and European intelligence-whistleblower frameworks contrast with the Middle East

### Takeaway
The US (ICWPA 1998, PPD-19 of 2012, codified in 2014) and the EU (Directive 2019/1937) both recognize intelligence whistleblowing as a legal category but channel it internally and withhold protection for public or media disclosure; the EU Directive expressly leaves national security and classified information to member states, and US law offers no public-interest defence to Espionage Act charges. The Middle Eastern states surveyed differ in kind, not degree: apart from Israel's labor-court and Ombudsman protections for non-classified "integrity" disclosures, none recognizes a protected disclosure category for security insiders at all, and several (Iran, Saudi Arabia, UAE, Egypt) treat unauthorized disclosure as a capital or terrorism offence.

### Cited Findings

**United States**
- The Whistleblower Protection Act 1989 "expressly excluded intelligence agency employees." The ICWPA (1998) "was limited to specifying a process for making a complaint and offered no specific protections"; it "does not explicitly prohibit retaliation"; CRS calls it "the oldest of the three and the least protective." — [CRS R45345, Intelligence Community Whistleblower Protections](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/R45345.html); [CRS R43765](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/R43765.html)
- ICWPA mechanics (50 U.S.C. § 3033(k)(5)): an "urgent concern" is "a serious or flagrant problem, abuse, violation of the law or Executive order, or deficiency relating to the funding, administration, or operation of an intelligence activity... involving classified information"; the IC Inspector General has 14 days to "determine whether the complaint or information appears credible," and the DNI must forward it to the intelligence committees "within 7 calendar days." The 2019 Ukraine complaint dispute (Acting DNI withheld; OLC opinion of 24 Sept 2019 agreed) showed the channel's dependence on executive cooperation. — [Schiff letter, 10 Sept 2019](https://irp.fas.org/congress/2019_cr/schiff-091019.pdf); [DOJ OLC opinion](https://www.justice.gov/olc/page/file/1205706/dl)
- Presidential Policy Directive 19, "Protecting Whistleblowers with Access to Classified Information" (10 October 2012): prohibits any officer with authority over personnel actions from taking or threatening action against an IC employee "as reprisal for a Protected Disclosure," and extends the bar to security-clearance decisions; agency IGs review alleged clearance reprisals and may recommend reconsideration. The FY2014 Intelligence Authorization Act codified it (50 U.S.C. § 3234); ICD 120 implements it; P.L. 115-118 (January 2018) extended some protections to contractors. — [PPD-19 text (GSA directives library)](https://www.gsa.gov/directives-library/protecting-whistleblowers-with-access-to-classified-information); [PPD-19 PDF](https://kkc.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/06/intelligence_presidential-policy-directive-19-oct-10-2012.pdf); [ICD 120](https://www.odni.gov/ICIG-Whistleblower/resources/ICD_120.pdf); [CRS R45345](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/R45345.html)
- CRS: the three measures "generally cover disclosures made through government channels, such as agency inspectors general or congressional intelligence committees, and none protects against retaliation or potential criminal liability for disclosures to media sources." — [CRS R45345](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/R45345.html)
- Espionage Act practice: between 1917 and 2009 "only one person was convicted under the Espionage Act for leaking to a news organization"; the Obama administration brought 8-10 leak prosecutions depending on counting; the first Trump administration at least four (Winner, Albury, Schulte, Hale). Prosecutors in the Drake case argued "the only intent required under the statute is that the defendant retained the documents willfully"; Hale's supporters said he was denied "the right to make a 'public interest defense'"; Hale received 45 months in July 2021. — [Freedom of the Press Foundation](https://freedom.press/news/obama-used-espionage-act-put-record-number-reporters-sources-jail-and-trump-could-be-even-worse/); [NBC News, 2019](https://www.nbcnews.com/politics/justice-department/under-trump-more-leaks-more-leak-investigations-n992121); [RSF on Hale](https://rsf.org/en/us-trump-administration-prosecutes-third-whistleblower-under-espionage-act); [Washington Times on Hale sentence](https://www.washingtontimes.com/news/2021/jul/28/daniel-hale-former-air-force-analyst-sentenced-to-/); [Knight First Amendment Institute tracker](https://knightcolumbia.org/reading-room/espionage-act)

**European Union and Council of Europe**
- Directive (EU) 2019/1937 (adopted 23 October 2019; OJ L 305, 26 Nov 2019; transposition deadline 17 December 2021): Article 3(2): "This Directive shall not affect the responsibility of Member States to ensure national security or their power to protect their essential security interests. In particular, it shall not apply to reports of breaches of the procurement rules involving defence or security aspects unless they are covered by the relevant acts of the Union." Article 3(3): the Directive "shall not affect the application of Union or national law relating to... (a) the protection of classified information; (b) the protection of legal and medical professional privilege; (c) the secrecy of judicial deliberations; (d) rules on criminal procedure." Recital 24: "National security remains the sole responsibility of each Member State." — [Luxgap, Article 3](https://luxgap.com/lois/whistleblowing/art-3/?lang=en); [Luxgap, Recital 24](https://luxgap.com/lois/whistleblowing/cons-24/?lang=en); [eucrim](https://eucrim.eu/news/whistleblowing-directive-published/); [Resource Centre on Media Freedom](https://www.rcmediafreedom.eu/Tools/Legal-Resources/The-new-EU-Directive-on-whistleblower-protection)
- During drafting, GAP objected that a Council draft "would have allowed intelligence agencies to ban any public freedom of expression" and that "no other whistleblower law permits those accused of misconduct the discretion to cancel protection for public disclosures." A Polish/EU comparative paper finds "the vague nature of the exception generates legal uncertainty" and that CJEU case law requires exclusions to rest on "a concrete threat to fundamental state interests." All 27 member states had transposition laws by 2025. — [GAP](https://whistleblower.org/?p=18427); [Researcher.life abstract, "When Security Overrides Transparency"](https://discovery.researcher.life/article/when-security-overrides-transparency-the-national-security-clause-in-whistleblower-protection-polish-and-eu-perspective/9b48bc3fcaa33698810336f35a572ae5); [EUROMIL whistleblowing survey 2023](https://euromil.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/2309_Report_Whistleblowing_Survey.pdf)
- Council of Europe CM/Rec(2014)7 contains a national-security carve-out; PACE asked for a binding instrument on "national security and intelligence" whistleblowers; the ECtHR nonetheless protected an intelligence insider in Bucur and Toma v. Romania (see KQ1). — [CoE Rec(2014)7](https://rm.coe.int/09000016805c5ea5); [PACE](https://pace.coe.int/en/files/21651/pdf); [ECtHR factsheet](https://www.echr.coe.int/documents/d/echr/FS_Whistleblowers_ENG)

### Inferences
- On a spectrum from "no recognition" to "protected public disclosure," the US and EU sit in the middle (recognized category, internal channels only, no public-interest defence); Israel sits just below (recognized category for integrity disclosures, none for classified disclosures, Supreme Court review of censorship); Turkey/Jordan/Lebanon/Iraq lower (anti-corruption channels only, security journalism criminalized); Iran, the Gulf and Egypt at the bottom (no category; disclosure equals terrorism/espionage/false news, with death available in Iran, Saudi Arabia and the UAE for undefined "national unity" or "corruption on earth" offences).
- The shared Western and Middle Eastern weakness is identical in structure: the receiving institution (IG/DNI; Israeli Comptroller; Saudi Public Prosecution; Lebanese NACC) is part of, or dependent on, the executive whose conduct is at issue. The 2019 US Ukraine episode and the 2026 Israeli Comptroller vacancy are usable illustrations that even "good" channels fail when politically captured.
- For the group paper, the Tshwane proviso ("provided that an effective mechanism exists") and the Guja "alternative channels" criterion give a principled basis for treating Middle Eastern direct-to-public or direct-to-foreign-court disclosures as legitimate whistleblowing even where equivalent US/EU acts would be unprotected.

### Gaps
- CRS reports were last updated 2021 in the sources found; later amendments (e.g., FY2023-2026 Intelligence Authorization Acts) were not checked.
- The Directive text was verified only through secondary reproductions (luxgap, betterregulation), not EUR-Lex, which was blocked.
- No official DOJ tally of Espionage Act leak prosecutions was found; counts are from press-freedom organizations and journalists.
