The official practice exam, worked and explained

Each part below has four pieces: what the question is really asking, which rule to use and why, the numbered steps with every line of arithmetic, and the mistake that usually costs the point. Read a part, close it, then redo it on paper from the question alone.

How points are earned: write the formula, substitute the numbers, show the arithmetic, box the answer. Round probabilities to four decimal places. A correct number with no work can lose credit; a wrong number with correct work can keep most of it.

Question 1 — Employment status and gender (6 points)

StatusMalesFemalesRow total
Full-time221840
Part-time61016
Unemployed325
Not in labor force51217
Column total364278

What this table is: 78 people, sorted two ways at once. Each inside cell counts the people who are both things (22 people are male AND full-time). The first thing to do on any table question, before reading part (a): write the row totals and the column totals in the margins. Every answer is then "a count ÷ the group you are picking from."

(a) What is the probability that a randomly selected resident is male? (1 pt)

Asking: out of everybody, what fraction are male?

Rule: single category → its total ÷ grand total.

  1. "Male" is the whole Males column, so the count is the column total, 36.
  2. The group you are picking from is all 78 residents.
  3. Divide.
P(male) = 36 / 78 = 0.4615

Answer: 0.4615

Watch out: 22 is only the full-time males. "Male" means the entire column.

(b) What is the probability that a randomly selected resident is part-time? (1 pt)

Asking: out of everybody, what fraction work part-time (men and women together)?

Rule: single category → its total ÷ grand total.

  1. Part-time is a row: 6 males + 10 females = 16.
  2. Divide by 78.
Part-time total = 6 + 10 = 16
P(part-time) = 16 / 78 = 0.2051

Answer: 0.2051

Watch out: add both cells in the row; using 6 or 10 alone answers a different question.

(c) What is the probability that a randomly selected resident is male OR unemployed? (1 pt)

Asking: what fraction are in either group — male, unemployed, or both?

Rule: OR → P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B). Adding the two groups counts the overlap twice, so subtract it once.

  1. P(male) = 36/78.
  2. P(unemployed) = row total 5 → 5/78.
  3. Overlap = people who are both: the male-unemployed cell = 3 → 3/78.
  4. Add the first two, subtract the overlap.
P(male or unemployed) = P(male) + P(unemployed) − P(male and unemployed)
= 36/78 + 5/78 − 3/78
= 38/78
= 0.4872

Answer: 0.4872

Watch out: forgetting to subtract the overlap gives 41/78 = 0.5256, which is wrong. Sanity check: count directly — all 36 males plus the 2 unemployed females = 38 people. Same answer.

(d) What is the probability that a randomly selected resident is female AND not in the labor force? (1 pt)

Asking: what fraction are both female and not in the labor force?

Rule: AND in a table → the single cell where the row and column meet, ÷ grand total.

  1. Find the cell: Females column, Not-in-labor-force row = 12.
  2. Divide by 78.
P(female and not in labor force) = 12 / 78 = 0.1538

Answer: 0.1538

Watch out: "and" never adds anything. It is one cell.

(e) What is the probability that a randomly selected resident is part-time given that the resident is female? (2 pts)

Asking: you already know the person is a woman. Among the women only, what fraction are part-time?

Rule: GIVEN → divide by the given group's total, not by 78. "Given female" shrinks the world to the 42 females.

  1. The given group is Females: total 42.
  2. Inside that group, the part-time count is the part-time-female cell: 10.
  3. Divide 10 by 42.
P(part-time | female) = N(part-time and female) / N(female)
= 10 / 42
= 0.2381

Answer: 0.2381

Watch out: 10/78 = 0.1282 is the classic error. Also notice the direction matters: P(female | part-time) would be 10/16 = 0.625, a different question. The word after "given" tells you the denominator.

Question 2 — Valid discrete probability distribution? (2 points)

x: 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 with P(x): 0.34, 0.21, 0.13, 0.04, 0.01. A probability distribution lists every value X can take and how likely each is. For it to be legitimate, two things must hold: no probability can be negative or above 1, and the probabilities must account for 100% of what can happen.

Is it valid? If not, why?

Rule: (1) each 0 ≤ P(x) ≤ 1, and (2) Σ P(x) = 1. Check both and say which fails.

  1. Rule 1: scan the list — 0.34, 0.21, 0.13, 0.04, 0.01 are all between 0 and 1. ✓
  2. Rule 2: add them.
0.34 + 0.21 + 0.13 + 0.04 + 0.01 = 0.73
0.73 ≠ 1

Answer: NOT a valid probability distribution — the probabilities add to 0.73, not 1 (27% of the outcomes are unaccounted for).

Watch out: a sum over 1 is just as invalid as a sum under 1, and a single negative entry makes a distribution invalid even if the sum is 1. Always write both checks; the question says "explain why."

Question 3 — Stanley Cup games (8 points)

x = number of games in the series: 4, 5, 6, 7 with frequencies 20, 20, 26, 17. These are counts of how many series ended in 4 games, 5 games, and so on. The question asks you to turn the counts into probabilities, picture them, and then describe the "typical" series (mean) and how much series vary (standard deviation).

(a) Construct a probability distribution for X. (2 pts)

Asking: what is the probability a series lasts 4 games? 5? 6? 7?

Rule: P(x) = count ÷ total (the empirical method from Chapter 4).

  1. Total number of series = 20 + 20 + 26 + 17 = 83.
  2. Divide each count by 83; round to 4 decimals.
  3. Check they add to about 1.
Total = 20 + 20 + 26 + 17 = 83
P(4) = 20/83 = 0.2410
P(5) = 20/83 = 0.2410
P(6) = 26/83 = 0.3133
P(7) = 17/83 = 0.2048
Check: 0.2410 + 0.2410 + 0.3133 + 0.2048 = 1.0001 ≈ 1 ✓ (rounding)

Answer: the table x = 4, 5, 6, 7 with P(x) = 0.2410, 0.2410, 0.3133, 0.2048

Watch out: the probabilities are counts ÷ 83, not counts ÷ 100 and not the counts themselves. A sum of 1.0001 is fine — it is rounding, not an error.

(b) Graph the discrete probability distribution. (2 pts)

Asking: draw the picture of part (a).

Rule: x on the horizontal axis, P(x) on the vertical axis; a vertical line (or bar) above each x whose height is its probability.

  1. Horizontal axis: mark 4, 5, 6, 7.
  2. Vertical axis: P(x), scaled from 0 to about 0.35.
  3. Above x = 4 draw a line up to 0.2410; above 5 up to 0.2410; above 6 up to 0.3133 (the tallest); above 7 up to 0.2048.
  4. Label both axes.
x:     4        5        6        7
P(x):  0.2410   0.2410   0.3133   0.2048   ← heights of the lines

Answer: four vertical lines/bars at 4, 5, 6, 7 with those heights; the tallest is at x = 6.

Watch out: the heights are the probabilities, not the frequencies (20, 20, 26, 17). Label the axes — that is part of the credit.

(c) Compute and interpret the mean of X. (2 pts)

Asking: on average, how many games does a series last? And say what that number means in words.

Rule: μ = Σ x·P(x). It is a weighted average: each value counts as much as its probability.

  1. Multiply each x by its P(x).
  2. Add the four products.
  3. Interpret: "over many series, the average is about ___ games."
μ = Σ x·P(x)
  = 4(0.2410) + 5(0.2410) + 6(0.3133) + 7(0.2048)
  = 0.9640 + 1.2050 + 1.8798 + 1.4336
  = 5.4824 ≈ 5.48 games

Answer: μ ≈ 5.48 games. Interpretation: if we looked at many Stanley Cup series, the average length would be about 5.48 games.

Watch out: the mean is NOT (4 + 5 + 6 + 7)/4 = 5.5. The probabilities are the weights; 6-game series happen more often, so they pull the mean. Write the interpretation sentence — it is part of the 2 points.

(d) Compute the standard deviation of X. (2 pts)

Asking: how far from 5.48 does a typical series land?

Rule: σ = √( Σ (x − μ)²·P(x) ). For each x: subtract the mean, square it, multiply by its probability; add; square root.

  1. Use μ = 5.48 from part (c).
  2. For each x compute (x − 5.48)², then multiply by P(x).
  3. Add the four results = the variance σ².
  4. Take the square root.
x = 4:  (4 − 5.48)² = (−1.48)² = 2.1904;  2.1904 × 0.2410 = 0.5279
x = 5:  (5 − 5.48)² = (−0.48)² = 0.2304;  0.2304 × 0.2410 = 0.0555
x = 6:  (6 − 5.48)² = ( 0.52)² = 0.2704;  0.2704 × 0.3133 = 0.0847
x = 7:  (7 − 5.48)² = ( 1.52)² = 2.3104;  2.3104 × 0.2048 = 0.4732
σ² = 0.5279 + 0.0555 + 0.0847 + 0.4732 = 1.1413
σ  = √1.1413 = 1.0683 ≈ 1.07 games

Answer: σ ≈ 1.07 games

Watch out: (1) don't stop at the variance 1.1413 — the question says standard deviation, so take the square root; (2) there is no "divide by n" here, the probabilities already do the averaging; (3) square the deviation before multiplying by P(x).

Question 4 — Driving age poll (8 points)

60% of U.S. women say the minimum driving age should be 18. Sample 10 women. Why this is binomial: a fixed number of trials (n = 10 women), each either says yes (success) or no, the women are chosen at random from a huge population so the trials are independent, and the chance of a yes is the same each time (p = 0.60). X = the number who say yes.

Set up once, use for every part: n = 10, p = 0.60, 1 − p = 0.40.  P(x) = ₁₀Cₓ (0.60)ˣ (0.40)¹⁰⁻ˣ

(a) Exactly 3 believe the minimum driving age should be 18. (2 pts)

Asking: probability that the count of yes-sayers is exactly 3 (and the other 7 say no).

Rule: binomial formula with x = 3. The three factors mean: (ways to choose which 3 say yes) × (those 3 say yes) × (the other 7 say no).

  1. ₁₀C₃ = 10! / (3!·7!) = (10·9·8)/(3·2·1) = 120.
  2. (0.60)³ = 0.216.
  3. (0.40)⁷ = 0.0016384.
  4. Multiply.
P(3) = ₁₀C₃ (0.60)³ (0.40)⁷
     = 120 × 0.216 × 0.0016384
     = 0.0425

Answer: 0.0425

Watch out: the exponent on 0.40 is n − x = 7, not 3. Keep the full decimals of 0.40⁷ until the end.

(b) Fewer than 3 believe the minimum driving age should be 18. (2 pts)

Asking: the count is 0, 1, or 2. "Fewer than 3" does not include 3.

Rule: add the separate probabilities P(0) + P(1) + P(2), each from the binomial formula.

  1. P(0): ₁₀C₀ = 1, (0.6)⁰ = 1, (0.4)¹⁰ = 0.0001049.
  2. P(1): ₁₀C₁ = 10, (0.6)¹ = 0.6, (0.4)⁹ = 0.0002621.
  3. P(2): ₁₀C₂ = 45, (0.6)² = 0.36, (0.4)⁸ = 0.0006554.
  4. Add.
P(0) = 1 × 1 × 0.0001049      = 0.0001
P(1) = 10 × 0.6 × 0.0002621   = 0.0016
P(2) = 45 × 0.36 × 0.0006554  = 0.0106
P(X < 3) = 0.0001 + 0.0016 + 0.0106 = 0.0123

Answer: 0.0123

Watch out: "fewer than 3" stops at 2. "At most 3" or "3 or fewer" would include P(3) too. Read the inequality word twice.

(c) Between 1 and 3 (inclusive) believe the minimum driving age should be 18. (2 pts)

Asking: the count is 1, 2, or 3. "Inclusive" means both ends count.

Rule: add P(1) + P(2) + P(3). You already have all three from parts (a) and (b).

  1. P(1) = 0.0016 and P(2) = 0.0106 from (b).
  2. P(3) = 0.0425 from (a).
  3. Add.
P(1 ≤ X ≤ 3) = P(1) + P(2) + P(3)
             = 0.0016 + 0.0106 + 0.0425
             = 0.0547

Answer: 0.0547

Watch out: don't include P(0). On the exam, reuse numbers you already computed — write "from part (a)" next to them.

(d) For a random sample of 200 U.S. women, find the expected number and standard deviation of those who believe the minimum driving age should be 18. (2 pts)

Asking: with a bigger sample, how many yes-sayers should we expect, and how much would that count typically vary?

Rule: binomial shortcuts μ = n·p and σ = √(n·p·(1 − p)). No formula table needed.

  1. n is now 200; p is still 0.60.
  2. μ = 200 × 0.60.
  3. σ = √(200 × 0.60 × 0.40).
μ = n·p = 200(0.60) = 120
σ = √(n·p·(1 − p)) = √(200 × 0.60 × 0.40) = √48 = 6.9282 ≈ 6.93

Answer: expected number 120, standard deviation ≈ 6.93

Watch out: the square root. Leaving the answer as 48 is the variance, not the SD. (Bonus sense check: counts between 120 ± 2(6.93) = 106 and 134 are "usual.")

Question 5 — Daily temperature, uniform (5 points)

Temperature is uniform between 70°F and 100°F: every temperature in that range is equally likely, so the probability curve is a flat rectangle. For a continuous variable, probability is area. The rectangle's total area must be 1, which fixes its height.

Set up once: a = 70, b = 100, width b − a = 30, height = 1/30. Probability of a piece = (length of the piece) ÷ 30.

(a) Sketch or describe the graph of the uniform pdf. (1 pt)

Asking: what does the probability curve look like, with numbers?

  1. Horizontal axis from 70 to 100.
  2. A flat line at height 1/(100 − 70) = 1/30 ≈ 0.0333 across that whole interval, 0 outside it.
  3. Check: area = width × height = 30 × 1/30 = 1.
height = 1/(b − a) = 1/(100 − 70) = 1/30
area = 30 × (1/30) = 1 ✓

Answer: a rectangle from 70 to 100 with height 1/30.

Watch out: state the height; "a flat line" alone is not a full answer.

(b) Find P(75 ≤ X ≤ 85). (2 pts)

Asking: what fraction of the rectangle sits between 75 and 85?

Rule: P(c ≤ X ≤ d) = (d − c) ÷ (b − a) — the piece's share of the total width.

P(75 ≤ X ≤ 85) = (85 − 75)/(100 − 70) = 10/30 = 0.3333

Answer: 0.3333

Watch out: no z-scores here — that is for the normal curve. The uniform is just length ÷ total width.

(c) Find P(X < 80). (2 pts)

Asking: what fraction of the rectangle is below 80?

Rule: same formula; the piece runs from the left end (70) up to 80.

P(X < 80) = (80 − 70)/(100 − 70) = 10/30 = 0.3333

Answer: 0.3333

Watch out: P(X ≤ 80) is the same number — a single point has zero width, so "less than" and "less than or equal" give the same area.

Question 6 — Normal distribution and z-scores (6 points)

The standard normal table (Table V) gives the area to the LEFT of a z value: the row is z to one decimal place, the column is the hundredths digit. The entry is the probability of being below that z. Everything else (right of, between) is built from "left of."

(a) Find the area to the left of z = −1.10. (2 pts)

Asking: exactly what the table gives.

  1. Row −1.1, column .00.
  2. Read the entry.
Area left of z = −1.10 → table (row −1.1, column .00) = 0.1357

Answer: 0.1357

Watch out: if your table only has positive z, use symmetry: left of −1.10 = 1 − (left of +1.10) = 1 − 0.8643 = 0.1357.

(b) Find the area between z = −0.60 and z = 1.45. (2 pts)

Asking: the strip of area between two z values.

Rule: BETWEEN = (left of the bigger z) − (left of the smaller z). The big left-area includes the strip; subtracting the small left-area removes everything below the strip.

  1. Left of 1.45: row 1.4, column .05 → 0.9265.
  2. Left of −0.60: row −0.6, column .00 → 0.2743.
  3. Subtract.
left of  1.45 = 0.9265
left of −0.60 = 0.2743
between = 0.9265 − 0.2743 = 0.6522

Answer: 0.6522

Watch out: subtract the areas, never the z values. And it is bigger-left minus smaller-left; if you get a negative number you subtracted the wrong way.

(c) X is normally distributed with μ = 80 and σ = 10. Find P(70 < X < 95). (2 pts)

Asking: same as (b), but first the x values must be turned into z values.

Rule: z = (x − μ)/σ for each endpoint, then BETWEEN as above.

  1. z for 70: (70 − 80)/10 = −1.00.
  2. z for 95: (95 − 80)/10 = 1.50.
  3. Table: left of −1.00 = 0.1587; left of 1.50 = 0.9332.
  4. Subtract.
z₁ = (70 − 80)/10 = −1.00 → area left = 0.1587
z₂ = (95 − 80)/10 =  1.50 → area left = 0.9332
P(70 < X < 95) = 0.9332 − 0.1587 = 0.7745

Answer: 0.7745

Watch out: compute both z's and show both table values on paper. Sanity check: 70 to 95 covers the middle of the curve (μ = 80), so a big probability like 0.77 makes sense.

Question 7 — Wechsler IQ scores (8 points)

IQ scores are normal with μ = 100, σ = 15. Three of the four parts are "forward": start from a score, convert to z, read the table. Part (d) is "backward": start from a percentage, find z inside the table, convert back to a score.

Forward: z = (x − 100)/15, round to 2 decimals → table → left / 1 − left / subtract.  Backward: area → z → x = 100 + z(15).

(a) Probability that a randomly selected test taker scores above 125. (2 pts)

Asking: the area to the RIGHT of 125.

Rule: convert to z; the table gives the LEFT area; right = 1 − left.

  1. z = (125 − 100)/15 = 25/15 = 1.6667 → round to 1.67.
  2. Table: left of 1.67 (row 1.6, column .07) = 0.9525.
  3. Right = 1 − 0.9525.
z = (125 − 100)/15 = 1.67
area left of 1.67 = 0.9525
P(X > 125) = 1 − 0.9525 = 0.0475

Answer: 0.0475

Watch out: the single most common error on the exam is writing 0.9525. Sanity check: 125 is above average, so fewer than half score above it — the answer must be under 0.5.

(b) Probability that a randomly selected test taker scores below 90. (2 pts)

Asking: the area to the LEFT of 90 — exactly what the table gives.

  1. z = (90 − 100)/15 = −10/15 = −0.6667 → −0.67.
  2. Table: left of −0.67 (row −0.6, column .07) = 0.2514.
z = (90 − 100)/15 = −0.67
P(X < 90) = area left of −0.67 = 0.2514

Answer: 0.2514

Watch out: keep the negative sign on z. Sanity check: 90 is below average, so the answer must be under 0.5. ✓

(c) Proportion of test takers who score between 110 and 140. (2 pts)

Asking: the strip of area between two scores.

Rule: two z's, two left-areas, bigger minus smaller.

  1. z for 110: (110 − 100)/15 = 0.67 → left = 0.7486.
  2. z for 140: (140 − 100)/15 = 2.67 → left = 0.9962.
  3. Subtract.
z₁ = (110 − 100)/15 = 0.67 → 0.7486
z₂ = (140 − 100)/15 = 2.67 → 0.9962
P(110 < X < 140) = 0.9962 − 0.7486 = 0.2476

Answer: 0.2476 (about 24.8% of test takers)

Watch out: "proportion" and "probability" are the same number here; write it as a decimal to 4 places.

(d) What intelligence score places a child in the 98th percentile? (2 pts)

Asking: the score that 98% of children are BELOW. You are given the area and must find the score — the reverse of (a)–(c).

Rule (backward): area to the left → find it INSIDE the table → read z off the edges → x = μ + z·σ.

  1. 98th percentile → area to the left = 0.98.
  2. Look inside the table for 0.98. The entries near it are 0.9798 (z = 2.05) and 0.9803 (z = 2.06); 0.9798 is closer, so z = 2.05.
  3. Convert back: x = 100 + 2.05 × 15.
area to the left = 0.98 → closest table entry 0.9798 → z = 2.05
x = μ + z·σ = 100 + 2.05(15) = 100 + 30.75 = 130.75 ≈ 131

Answer: about 131 (130.75)

Watch out: do not put 0.98 into the z formula — 0.98 is an area, not a score. Backward problems use the table in reverse: find the area in the body, read z from the margins, then x = μ + zσ. Sanity check: the 98th percentile must be well above 100. ✓

The pattern across the whole exam

  • Q1 is the only "words → rule" question: or / and / given. The rest are "set up → formula → arithmetic."
  • Q3 and Q4 are long but mechanical; the points are in showing each product and each sum.
  • Q5 has no z-scores. Q6 and Q7 are nothing but z-scores and table reads; (d) is the only backward step.
  • Every numeric answer: four decimals, boxed, with the formula written above it.
