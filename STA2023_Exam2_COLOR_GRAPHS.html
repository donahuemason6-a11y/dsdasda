EXAM 1 SKILLS THAT COME BACK

  • Mean = add everything, divide by how many (the balance point). μ = Σx ÷ N, x̄ = Σx ÷ n
  • Median = sort, take the middle (average the two middles if even count); resistant to outliers — the mean is not. Mode = most frequent value(s); can be more than one; none if nothing repeats.
  • Standard deviation = on average, how far values are from the mean. Small SD = values huddle near the mean; big SD = spread out. Why the recipe: the gaps (x − mean) always add to 0, so square them (all positive), average the squares (= variance), then √ to get back to normal units (= SD).
σ² = Σ(x − μ)² ÷ N  (population)  ·  s² = Σ(x − x̄)² ÷ (n − 1)  (sample)  ·  SD = √variance
"sample" → divide by n − 1  ·  calculator: σx = population SD, Sx = sample SD
  1. Find the mean. 2. Each value − mean. 3. Square each. 4. Add. 5. ÷ N or ÷ (n − 1). 6. √.
Q: "Sample 18, 22, 25, 28, 30: sample variance and SD?" A: x̄ = 123/5 = 24.6; gaps −6.6, −2.6, 0.4, 3.4, 5.4; squares 43.56 + 6.76 + 0.16 + 11.56 + 29.16 = 91.20; s² = 91.20/4 = 22.8; s = √22.8 = 4.77.
  • z-score = how many SDs away from the mean; + above, − below. Lets you compare different scales: the bigger z did relatively better. z = (x − μ) ÷ σ ACT 27 (μ 18, σ 6): z = 1.50 beats SAT 1200 (μ 1000, σ 200): z = 1.00. If lower is better (race times), the smaller z did better. z below −2 or above 2 → unusual.
  • Empirical Rule (bell-shaped data only): 68% of values within 1 SD of the mean, 95% within 2, 99.7% within 3; the pieces are 34 | 13.5 | 2.35 | 0.15 on each side. μ = 29.8, σ = 6.4: 95% between 29.8 ± 12.8 = 17.0 and 42.6; IQ 85 to 130 = 34 + 34 + 13.5 = 81.5%.
  • Quartiles cut sorted data into quarters: Q₂ = median, Q₁/Q₃ = medians of the lower/upper halves (odd n → leave the median out). IQR = Q₃ − Q₁; lower fence = Q₁ − 1.5·IQR, upper fence = Q₃ + 1.5·IQR; outlier = below the lower fence or above the upper fence

CH 4 — PROBABILITY

What probability is

  • Probability = the long-run fraction of times something happens (flip a coin a million times → about half heads → 0.5). 0 ≤ P(E) ≤ 1; all outcomes together add to 1. Unusual event = probability less than 0.05.
  • Sample space S = the list of everything that could happen; an event = any part of that list you care about. Two coin flips: S = {HH, HT, TH, TT}.
  • Classical (all outcomes equally likely — dice, cards, coins): just count. P(E) = N(E) ÷ N(S) P(two heads) = 1/4 = 0.25; P(at least one tail) = 3/4.
  • Empirical (from data you collected): how often it happened. P(E) ≈ frequency ÷ total trials 22 of 200 carpool → 0.11; 5 walk → 0.025 < 0.05 → unusual.
  • Subjective = an educated guess when there's no data (chance of a recession).
  • Probability model = list of outcomes with probabilities; valid only if each is 0–1 and they add to 1. {A 0.40, B 0.20} → not valid (sum 0.60); a −0.10 → not valid.
  • Disjoint (= mutually exclusive) = no outcomes in common, can't both happen (a card can't be a king AND a queen), so P(E and F) = 0. Independent = E happening does not change the probability of F (two coin flips; two different drivers). Disjoint is NOT independent: if E and F are disjoint and both possible, knowing E happened means F didn't → dependent.

The rules — spot the keyword

NOT:  P(Ec) = 1 − P(E)
OR:  P(E or F) = P(E) + P(F) − P(E and F)   (disjoint → the overlap is 0, just add)
AND, independent:  P(E and F) = P(E) · P(F)
AND, general:  P(E and F) = P(E) · P(F | E)
GIVEN:  P(F | E) = P(E and F)P(E) = N(E and F)N(E)
AT LEAST ONE:  P(at least 1) = 1 − P(none)
Independent?  check P(F | E) = P(F). Equal → independent; different → dependent
  • Why subtract in OR: adding P(E) + P(F) counts the overlap twice, so take it out once.
  • Why "given" changes things: knowing E shrinks the world to only the cases where E happened, so you divide by E's total, not the grand total. P(6) = 1/6, but P(6 | even) = 1/3 because only {2, 4, 6} remain.
  • "At least one" has many cases, so compute the one case you don't want (none) and subtract from 1.
  • Independence rule of thumb: sampling without replacement from a big population — if the sample is under 5% of the population, treat the picks as independent.
Q: "P(speeder pulled over) = 0.8, P(ticket | pulled over) = 0.9. P(pulled over AND ticket)?" A: 0.8 × 0.9 = 0.72
Q: "P(24-yr-old male survives the year) = 0.9986. P(two both survive)? P(at least one of 1000 dies)?" A: 0.9986² = 0.9972; 1 − 0.99861000 = 1 − 0.2464 = 0.7536
Q: "One card: P(king or diamond)?" A: 4/52 + 13/52 − 1/52 = 16/52 = 0.3077 (the king of diamonds was counted twice). "P(king or queen)?" disjoint → 4/52 + 4/52 = 0.1538
Q: "52% played the lottery; P(has not)?" A: 1 − 0.52 = 0.48
Q: "Pick 2 of 4 friends (A, B, S, D): P(A and B)? P(B goes)?" A: S = {AB, AS, AD, BS, BD, SD} → 1/6; 3/6 = 0.5
Q: "Are 'widowed' and 'female' independent? P(widowed) = 0.0552, P(widowed | female) = 11.5/138.1 = 0.0833." A: 0.0833 ≠ 0.0552 → dependent

Two-way (contingency) table ★Q1

A table of counts split two ways (status × gender). Probabilities are just cells or totals divided by the right denominator.

  1. Add row totals and column totals; grand total = N.
  2. "is A" → A's total ÷ N.
  3. "A or B" → P(A) + P(B) − P(both) (the overlap cell).
  4. "A and B" → the one cell ÷ N.
  5. "A given B" → cell ÷ B's total (divide by the GIVEN group only).
Q: "Employment status of 78 residents: Full-time M 22 / F 18; Part-time M 6 / F 10; Unemployed M 3 / F 2; Not in labor force M 5 / F 12. Males = 36, Females = 42."
Row totals: full-time 40, part-time 16, unemployed 5, not in labor force 17.
(a) P(male) = 36/78 = 0.4615
(b) P(part-time) = 16/78 = 0.2051
(c) P(male OR unemployed) = 36/78 + 5/78 − 3/78 = 38/78 = 0.4872
(d) P(female AND not in labor force) = 12/78 = 0.1538
(e) P(part-time | female) = 10/42 = 0.2381

Counting — "how many ways"

  • A sequence of choices (first this, then that): multiply the number of options at each step. 3-letter airport codes: 26·26·26 = 17,576; chair, vice-chair, secretary from 14 people: 14·13·12 = 2,184.
  • Permutation = picking r of n where ORDER MATTERS (1st/2nd/3rd place, arranging in a line). nPr = n!(n − r)! 7P5 = 7!/2! = 2,520; 10 runners, top 3: 10P3 = 10·9·8 = 720; 7 schools in order: 7! = 5,040.
  • Combination = picking r of n where order DOESN'T matter (a committee, a hand of cards, a sample). nCr = n!r!(n − r)! 4C2 = 24/(2·2) = 6; samples of 4 from 20: 20C4 = (20·19·18·17)/24 = 4,845; P(4 cards all same rank) = 13/52C4 = 13/270,725 ≈ 0.00005.

CH 5 — DISCRETE RANDOM VARIABLES ★Q2 ★Q3

  • Random variable X = the number that comes out of a chance experiment (games in a series, profit from a bet, # who say yes). Discrete = you count it (0, 1, 2, …: number of pieces, people, heads); continuous = you measure it (time, mass, temperature — any decimal).
  • Probability distribution = a table of every value X can take with its probability (or a graph / formula).
VALID only if (1) every P(x) is between 0 and 1 AND (2) Σ P(x) = 1
Mean (expected value): μX = E(X) = Σ [ x · P(x) ]
SD: σX = √( Σ [ (x − μ)² · P(x) ] )    shortcut √( Σ [ x² · P(x) ] − μ² )
  • Mean of a distribution = a weighted average: each value counts as much as its probability. It's the long-run average per trial ("over many games you lose 8¢ a game on average").
  • SD of a distribution = same idea as before (typical distance from the mean) but each squared gap is weighted by its probability instead of averaged.
  1. From counts: P(x) = count ÷ total. Check the P(x) add to 1.
  2. Mean: multiply each x by its P(x); add.
  3. SD: (x − μ)² for each x; times P(x); add; √.
  4. Graph: a vertical line (or bar) above each x, height = P(x); x across, P(x) up.
Q: "Is this a valid discrete probability distribution? x: 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 with P(x): 0.34, 0.21, 0.13, 0.04, 0.01." A: each P(x) is 0–1 ✓, but 0.34 + 0.21 + 0.13 + 0.04 + 0.01 = 0.73 ≠ 1 → NOT valid.

CH 5 (cont.) — BINOMIAL ★Q4

Binomial experiment = the same yes/no trial repeated: (1) a FIXED number of trials n, (2) trials independent, (3) only two outcomes each time (success / failure), (4) the SAME p every trial. X = how many successes. Not binomial if n isn't fixed ("keep going until…") or picks are without replacement from a small group (p changes).

P(x) = nCx · px · (1 − p)n − x    nCx = n!x!(n − x)!
μ = n·p     σ = √( n·p·(1 − p) )     Excel =BINOM.DIST(x, n, p, FALSE) exactly; TRUE = P(X ≤ x)
Unusual count (when np(1 − p) ≥ 10): x below μ − 2σ or above μ + 2σ

Read the formula as: (number of ways to place the x successes among the n trials) × (probability of the x successes) × (probability of the n − x failures).

at least k / ≥ k1 − [P(0) + … + P(k − 1)]

Q: "60% of U.S. women say the minimum driving age should be 18. Random sample of 10. (a) Exactly 3? (b) Fewer than 3? (c) Between 1 and 3 inclusive? (d) Sample of 200: expected number and SD?"
n = 10, p = 0.60, 1 − p = 0.40
(a) P(3) = 10C3(0.6)3(0.4)7 = 120 · 0.216 · 0.0016384 = 0.0425
(b) P(0) = (0.4)10 = 0.0001; P(1) = 10(0.6)(0.4)9 = 0.0016; P(2) = 45(0.36)(0.4)8 = 0.0106 → P(X < 3) = 0.0123
(c) P(1) + P(2) + P(3) = 0.0016 + 0.0106 + 0.0425 = 0.0547
(d) μ = 200(0.60) = 120; σ = √(200 · 0.60 · 0.40) = √48 = 6.93. "Would 130 be unusual?" 120 ± 2(6.93) = 106.1 to 133.9 → 130 is inside → not unusual.
Q: "Binomial or not? (1) Roll a pair of dice 10 times, count 7s. (2) Select flights until 10 late ones are found. (3) Pick 4 of 30 students, count females." A: (1) YES: n = 10, p = 6/36 = 1/6. (2) NO: n not fixed. (3) NO: without replacement from 30 (4/30 = 13% > 5%) → not independent.

CH 6 — CONTINUOUS & NORMAL

Area = probability

  • A continuous variable has infinitely many values, so you can't list them. Instead, probability = AREA under a curve over an interval. The curve is the probability density function (pdf): never below 0, total area = 1.
  • P(X = one exact value) = 0 (a single point has no width), so P(a < X < b) = P(a ≤ X ≤ b).

Uniform distribution ★Q5

Uniform on [a, b] = a flat rectangle: every stretch of the same length is equally likely (friend is as likely to be 3–4 min late as 25–26 min late). Probability of an interval = its share of the total width.

height = 1b − a     P(c ≤ X ≤ d) = d − cb − a     value with area P to its LEFT: x = a + P·(b − a)
Q: "Daytime temperature X is uniformly distributed between 70°F and 100°F. (a) Sketch/describe the pdf. (b) P(75 ≤ X ≤ 85)? (c) P(X < 80)?"
(a) rectangle from 70 to 100, height 1/(100 − 70) = 1/30 (area 30 × 1/30 = 1)
(b) (85 − 75)/30 = 10/30 = 0.3333    (c) (80 − 70)/30 = 10/30 = 0.3333
Q: "Friend is 0–30 min late, uniform. P(10 to 20 min late)? 20% chance they arrive within the next ___ min?" A: 10/30 = 0.3333; 0 + 0.20(30) = 6 min (70–100°F: coolest 20% → 70 + 0.20(30) = 76°F). "Random number 0–10: P(between 2 and 5)? P(exactly 4.35)?" A: 3/10 = 0.3; 0.

Normal distribution ★Q6 ★Q7

Normal = the bell curve: symmetric around μ, most values near the middle, fewer far out. Every normal curve has the same shape — μ slides it left/right, σ makes it wider (big σ) or narrower (small σ).

Properties: (1) symmetric about μ; (2) mean = median = mode = μ, the highest point; (3) inflection points at μ ± σ; (4) total area = 1; (5) tails get closer to the axis but never touch; (6) Empirical Rule (picture below); (7) half the area (0.5) lies on each side of μ.

Standard normal Z = the normal curve with μ = 0, σ = 1. The trick of the chapter: you can't have a table for every μ and σ, so convert your value to a z-score and use ONE table (Table V), which gives the area to the LEFT of z.

z = x − μσ  (round z to 2 decimals)      x = μ + z·σ
LEFT ("less than / below"): table value  ·  RIGHT ("more than / above"): 1 − table value
BETWEEN x₁ and x₂: (left of z₂) − (left of z₁)  ·  negative z: left of −z = 1 − (left of +z)
  1. Draw a bell (see pictures), write μ under the peak, mark the x value(s), shade the region asked.
  2. z = (x − μ)/σ for each x, 2 decimals.
  3. Table: row = first two digits (1.6), column = last digit (.07) → area to the LEFT.
  4. Left → done. Right → 1 − area. Between → subtract the two lefts.

Backwards — find x from a percent / percentile ★Q7(d)

Now the area is GIVEN and you want the value. Reverse the steps: area → z (from inside the table) → x = μ + zσ. "k-th percentile" = the value with k% of the data below it, so area to the left = k/100 (5th → 0.05, 20th → 0.20, 98th → 0.98).

  1. Draw & shade; turn the words into an area to the LEFT ("top 10%" → left area 0.90; "bottom 5%" → 0.05; "middle 80%" → 0.10 and 0.90).
  2. Find that area INSIDE the table (closest value); read z from the row and column. Exactly halfway between two entries → average the two z's (0.95 → 1.645, 0.995 → 2.575).
  3. x = μ + z·σ.
Percentile → z:  1st −2.33 · 2nd −2.05 · 2.5th −1.96 · 5th −1.645 · 10th −1.28 · 20th −0.84 · 25th −0.67 · 50th 0
75th 0.67 · 80th 0.84 · 90th 1.28 · 95th 1.645 · 97.5th 1.96 · 98th 2.05 · 99th 2.33  (top 10% cutoff = 90th percentile)
Middle 80% ↔ z = ±1.28 · middle 90% ↔ ±1.645 · middle 95% ↔ ±1.96 · middle 99% ↔ ±2.575

Interpreting an area · Is it normal?

  • "Area to the right of 200 is 0.2903" means BOTH: the proportion of the population above 200 is 0.2903, AND the probability that one randomly chosen person is above 200 is 0.2903.
  • Assessing normality: histogram bell-shaped, one peak, roughly symmetric, or a normal probability plot close to a straight line → approximately normal. Restaurant A (long right tail) → skewed, not normal; Restaurant B (symmetric hump) → normal.

DON'T LOSE POINTS

  • "Or" → subtract the overlap once. "And" → the one cell. "Given" → divide by the given group, not the grand total.
  • Check Σ P(x) = 1 before calling a distribution valid; probabilities must be 0–1.
  • Binomial μ = np, σ = √(np(1−p)) — don't forget the √. Mean of a distribution is Σ x·P(x), not the average of the x's.
  • "Fewer than 3" stops at 2; "at least 3" = 1 − P(≤ 2); "at most" includes the number.
  • Uniform: probability = length ÷ total width; never use z. Normal: ALWAYS convert to z (2 decimals); table = area to the LEFT.
  • "Above / more than" → 1 − left. "Between" → big area minus small area. Negative z → 1 − (positive entry).
  • Percentile → area → z (from inside the table) → x = μ + zσ. "Top 10%" means left area 0.90.
  • Round probabilities to 4 decimals; write the formula every time.
